# Chapter 1 — The Cold Road

*Lu Yan, age 6 · Spirit Apprentice (rank 0, pre-awakening → awakening) · 0 rings · POV: Lu Yan · Slagford, western frontier of the Star Luo Empire*

---

Chen Mo died the way most people do — without ceremony, in the wrong place, over
nothing.

There had been ice on the overpass and a delivery he could have refused. There had
been the particular orange of sodium streetlights on wet asphalt, a color he had
seen ten thousand nights and never once thought of as the last thing he would see.
There had been a horn, very loud and very brief. Then there had been a long quiet
that was not sleep, because in sleep some part of you keeps watch, and in this quiet
nothing watched at all.

He had read enough stories to know what was supposed to happen next. A voice. A
system panel glowing in the dark. A god with an apology and a contract. He waited
for it with the mild, detached curiosity that had been his defining trait in life —
the temperament of a man who read about other people's adventures because he had
never been able to feel his own with any urgency.

Nothing came. The quiet simply thinned, the way fog thins, until it was warmth
instead of cold, and pressure instead of weightlessness, and then a scream that he
eventually understood was his own.

That was the cruelty of it, and the mercy. No tutorial. No second chances dangled
and withdrawn. Just the slow, undignified, total work of being born again into a
body that could not yet hold its own head up, in a world where the sky was the wrong
color at dusk — too red, banked like a forge left to cool.

He had time, lying in that first crib of dried grass and rough wool, to think about
what he had lost. He found, examining it carefully, that he had lost very little.
A small apartment. A job moving boxes from one shelf to another. A handful of people
who would be sad in proportion to how often they had remembered he existed, which
was not often. He turned the grief over in his hands like a stone, looking for the
sharp edge that should have cut him, and could not find one.

*That should worry me,* he thought, in the wordless soup of an infant's mind. *A
man who can lose his whole life and feel only mild interest is a man with something
already missing.*

He filed the worry away. He would have years to examine it. As it turned out, he
would spend most of his life pretending he never had.

---

They named him Lu Yan.

The *Lu* was his mother's, the only thing she gave him before the coughing sickness
took her in his second winter — a frontier surname, plain as dirt, meaning nothing
grander than *land*. The *Yan* was the midwife's small joke, or maybe her small
prayer: *flame*, for a baby born blue and silent who should have died and then,
without warning, opened his mouth and howled hot enough to fog the cold air. The
old women said a child who fought that hard to breathe would either burn bright or
burn out. They said it the way frontier people say everything — as a coin-toss, not
a blessing.

Slagford was not a place that produced blessings.

It crouched at the western hem of the Star Luo Empire where the maps stopped being
confident and started using words like *approximately*. A smelting town, a caravan
stop, a scab of stone and slag-heap built where two bad roads met and neither one
went anywhere good. To the east, eventually, were the heartlands and the cities and
the great Academies he knew only as names from a book he had read in another life.
To the west was the Ashwood, and past the Ashwood the wildlands, and in the
wildlands the soul beasts that everyone in Slagford fed their dead to and their
living, when the tithe came due.

By the time he was old enough to walk the slag-paths without falling, Lu Yan
understood three things about his second world with the cold completeness of a man
who had read its instruction manual and now lived inside the machine.

The first was that this was the Douluo Continent, and he knew its rules. Martial
souls. Spirit rings torn from the corpses of beasts. The long ladder of ranks that
turned a gutter child into a god if the child climbed hard enough and the ladder did
not throw him off. He knew the shape of it the way you know a song you have not
heard in years — not every word, but the melody, and where the dangerous notes were.

The second was that knowing the rules was almost worthless if you were starving.
Knowledge was a lever, and a lever needed a place to stand. He had no place to stand.
He had a dead mother, a dead father he had never met, a charity cot in the back of
the tallhouse where the town's one registered soul master let orphans sleep in
exchange for sweeping, and a body that had to grow up one slow, vulnerable year at a
time before it could carry any of the things he knew how to do.

The third thing he understood was the one he tried hardest not to.

He did not feel things the way the others did.

He watched the other orphans love and hate and fear with a kind of anthropological
patience, taking notes. When Mei Sang cried because the cold had killed the
sparrow she'd been keeping warm in her sleeve, he watched the way her whole small
body folded around the grief, the way it owned her, and he could name every part of
it and feel none of it. When Ke Lang, older and meaner, broke a younger boy's finger
to take his bread, Lu Yan felt no anger — only a clean, quick calculation: *Ke Lang
is strong, undisciplined, and afraid of the dark. Useful, eventually. Not now.*

He had been a mild man in his first life. He had thought the mildness was peace.
Lying awake in the tallhouse loft, listening to the other children breathe, he began
to suspect it had been the same missing thing all along, and that whatever was
broken in him had followed him across the gap between worlds, into a place where the
broken parts of a person were not punished but *rewarded*.

He was almost right.

He was wrong about which direction the brokenness ran.

---

The awakening came in the autumn of his sixth year, on the morning the frost first
held past noon.

Every child in the Empire was tested at six — that much was law, even out here where
the law arrived late and tired. Twice a year a name came down from the tallhouse and
the children gathered in the cold yard behind it, where the slag had been cleared to
bare rock and a single basin of grey crystal sat on an iron tripod, older than the
town, hauled west generations ago and never moved since. The **Spirit-Awakening
Crystal**. Cheap, by the standards of the heartlands. Out here it was the most
valuable object for two hundred li, and it was guarded by an old man who had been
told to guard it and a tally of debts no orphan could ever repay.

Lu Yan stood in line with eleven other children and waited his turn with the
stillness that had already started to make the adults uneasy.

Mei Sang was ahead of him, hands knotted in her sleeves, breath ghosting fast. When
the crystal lit for her it lit soft — a low green, the green of new leaves and damp
moss, and a faint coil of pale grass-mist rose from her palms and smelled, absurdly,
of summer. A **healing soul.** The examiner, a thin clerk sent up from the
prefecture for the season, made a small approving note. *Mistgrass*, he said. *Rank
two. Modest. Useful. Spirit Hall buys healers.* Mei Sang's face did something
complicated and frightened at the word *buys*. Lu Yan filed that too.

Ke Lang's crystal lit a dull, angry orange, and his martial soul came up his arm
as a coat of coarse grey bristles, a beast-soul, some kind of boar. Rank three. The
examiner's note was longer. Frontier soldiers came from boys like that.

Then it was Lu Yan's turn, and the morning, as it turned out, ended.

He put his hand to the crystal because that was what you did. He had spent six years
preparing for this moment in the cold theater of his own skull, rehearsing
indifference, planning for any of a hundred ordinary outcomes. A beast-soul he could
hide. A tool-soul he could use. A weak rank he could conceal and a strong rank he
could conceal harder, because in a place like Slagford a strong rank did not mean
opportunity, it meant you became the tithe instead of paying it.

He had not planned for the crystal to scream.

That was the only word for the sound it made — a high, splitting note that was not
quite sound, that he felt in his teeth and the wet of his eyes. The grey crystal
flushed. Not green, not orange, not the white or gold or pale violet of the
prefecture clerk's neat chart of ranks. It went *red*. A deep, arterial, furnace red
that climbed from the crystal's heart and filled it like a struck vein filling a
cup, until the old stone basin glowed like a coal and the frost on the iron tripod
hissed away to steam.

And behind Lu Yan, rising out of nothing in the cold yard, something built itself
in the air.

He felt it before he turned to look — felt it the way you feel someone step up
close behind you in the dark. A weight. A heat that did not warm. When he turned his
head, slow, deliberate, refusing on principle to flinch, he saw it taking shape out
of the red light: a thing of black iron and molten seams, broad as a millstone and
taller than a man, a great squat **brazier** or altar or forge-mouth, its interior
churning with a light that was not fire and behaved like fire and was, he would
later understand, neither. Around its rim were channels worn smooth, the way stone
is worn smooth by a thousand years of the same liquid running the same path. It hung
in the air a handspan above the frozen rock and turned, very slowly, as if to look
at the assembled town with no face at all.

The examiner had come halfway out of his chair. His brush had fallen. His mouth was
open on no word.

And into Lu Yan's right hand, unbidden, settled a weight.

A weapon. A war-maul — a great two-handed thing of the same black iron, its head a
rough block of what looked like cooling slag, veined with the same dull red, far too
heavy for the arm of a six-year-old to lift and lifting anyway, light as a willow
switch, because the strength to hold it did not come from his arm. It came from the
brazier. It came from the part of him the crystal had just dragged up into the
light, the part he had carried across the gap between two deaths and never named.

He knew this thing.

That was the worst of it, standing in the screaming red light with a god's hammer in
a child's fist. He *knew* it. Not from this world — there was nothing like it in any
chart in any prefecture, the examiner's stricken face said as much — but from the
other one. From a book he had read on a train, on a couch, in a life that had ended
under a sodium light. A pathway of power he had followed page by page in a story
about a different world entirely: the **Red Priest.** War and fire and plunder and
the long red road of sacrifice. The crucible that took what you fed it and gave back
strength, and took, for its strength, a price in the only currency that ever
mattered — your own mind, your own self, fed slowly into the furnace until the
warrior was gone and only the war was left.

That was the pathway. That was its drawback, written in every line of the story he
had loved: *you will become a monster.* Power without a leash, that ate the hand
that fed it.

Lu Yan stood in the cold yard with the brazier turning at his back and the maul warm
in his fist and the whole town frozen around him in fear, and he reached inward,
carefully, the way you reach into a drawer you know has a knife in it, looking for
the madness.

It was not there.

He searched. He was thorough — he was always thorough. The war-trance was there: he
could feel it lying just under his skin like a banked coal, the promise that if he
let it rise his reflexes would sharpen and his strength would double and pain would
stop being a thing that could reach him. In the story, that was the doorway to the
frenzy. That was where reason drowned. He took hold of it, deliberately, a child
gripping a live wire to see what it did —

— and the world went very clear, and very cold, and very *quiet.*

No rage. No red wash across his thoughts, no hunger snarling up to eat his sense of
himself. The opposite. The trance did not flood him; it *stilled* him, the way deep
water is still, the way the inside of a forge is still in the half-second before the
bellows blow. He stood in the cold yard and felt his own pulse slow, felt his
breathing drop to nothing, felt every detail of the morning arrange itself in front
of him with cut-glass exactness — the examiner's dropped brush, the angle of the
frightened guards' shoulders, the eleven children and which way each would run, Mei
Sang's wide green eyes, Ke Lang's bristling fear, the precise weight of the maul and
exactly where, if he chose, he could put it.

The power had come. The price had not.

A more emotional man might have laughed, or wept, or fallen to his knees in some
appropriate transport of fortune. Lu Yan stood very still and did the arithmetic,
because the arithmetic was the only thing he had ever been able to feel with any
urgency, and the answer it gave him was so large it took even him a moment to hold
the whole of it.

*The pathway without the price. The furnace that does not eat the one who feeds it.*

*I can climb this with nothing in me to throw me off.*

It did not occur to him, in that first clean cold moment, to ask the question that
mattered.

If the furnace does not take its price from the one who feeds it — then who pays?

---

He would have an answer before the morning was over. The world was generous that way.

The screaming had drawn the rats.

Not rats as his first world made them. Slagford sat on the lip of soul-beast country,
and even its vermin had a touch of the wild in them — **ash-rats**, grey and
quick and the size of cats, with a hard coppery sheen to their fur where the spirit
power had soaked into the line over uncounted generations. They denned in the
slag-heaps and they fed on the town's leavings and they were, individually, a
nuisance and not a danger. The crystal's not-quite-sound had flushed a dozen of them
out of the heaps in a panic, and they boiled across the cold yard in a grey wave,
and one of them — wounded, maybe, or simply stupid with fear — ran straight across
the toes of Lu Yan's bare frostbitten foot.

His hand moved before his thought did.

Not the maul. The maul was for armies; the part of him the crystal had woken knew
the difference already, knew it the way you know how to close your hand. He simply
*reached*, with something that was not his arm, and caught the ash-rat against the
frozen rock with a flat of crimson light, and pinned it.

It shrilled. It thrashed. Its small wild life beat against his grip like a moth
against a lamp.

And the brazier behind him *opened.*

He felt the channels run. He felt the worn smooth grooves around the crucible's rim
find their purpose, felt them reach out along the line of crimson light into the
pinned and struggling animal, and felt them begin, gently, the way water finds the
lowest path, to *drink.*

It did not hurt the rat at first. That was the part he would remember. There was no
violence in it, no tearing — the way there is no violence in a candle going out. The
ash-rat's struggling slowed. Its coppery sheen dulled. The frantic heat of its small
life thinned and ran backward along the crimson channel, into the crucible, into
*him*, and where it went he felt — for the first time since he had taken the maul,
the first time, perhaps, since the sodium light — he felt *warm.*

Not metaphorically. The cold that had lived in his bones for six frontier winters
receded a finger's width. The hollow under his breastbone, the missing place he had
carried between two worlds, filled — only slightly, only for a moment, but it
filled. The rat's life was small and it did not fill him for long. But it filled
him, and it was the most that empty place had ever held, and the part of Lu Yan that
did the arithmetic noted, with cold precision, that he wanted more.

The rat died. It did not take long.

He let the crimson light go and the small grey body dropped to the rock, weightless,
emptied, its eyes gone to dull beads. And in the half-circle of frozen ground where
it had fallen, where the channels of his crucible had reached — the frost did not
just steam away. The thin brown autumn grass that had pushed up between the slag,
the hardy frontier grass that survived the cold and the soot and everything else
Slagford could do to it, the grass that was the toughest living thing in that whole
hard yard —

— it browned, and curled, and died.

A perfect circle of it, an arm's length across, gone grey and brittle as if a
year of drought had been poured into it in a single breath. The life he had taken
from the rat had not all reached him. Some of it the crucible had spent simply
*opening* — and the cost of opening it had been paid, not by Lu Yan, who felt only
warmth, but by everything small and living close enough to reach.

He crouched there in the screaming red morning, six years old, a dead rat in front
of him and a ring of dead grass around him and a furnace turning at his back, and he
looked at the circle of brown for a long, long time.

The price had not vanished.

It had moved.

*Who pays,* he had failed to ask. Here was the answer, written in dead grass: *not
you. Never you. That is the whole horror of it. The world pays. Whatever is nearest
and weakest and cannot stop you — that pays.*

A different man, a whole man, might have recoiled. Might have felt the cold weight of
it settle into his chest and stay there, the first stone in a wall he would spend
the rest of his life either building or tearing down.

Lu Yan felt the warmth in the hollow under his breastbone, already fading, already
leaving him colder than before by exactly the amount it had given — the furnace's
true accounting, he would learn, was never in his favor, only in his *comfort* — and
he understood the shape of the rest of his life in that one clear cold instant, the
way he had understood the world from his crib.

He could climb this ladder higher and faster than anyone alive, because he had been
handed the one martial soul whose price he did not have to pay.

And every rung would be paid for by someone else.

He stood up. He brushed the frost from his knees with a child's small hands. He
looked, once, at the dead circle of grass — filing it, the way he filed everything,
in the cold cabinet of his mind under a heading he would not let himself read again
for years: *Cost. Displaced. Real.*

Then he turned to face the town that was staring at him as if he were a fire that
had learned to walk, and he made the first ruthless decision of his second life,
which was to let them keep being afraid, because a frightened town was a town that
left an orphan alone.

The examiner's voice, when it finally came, was not steady.

"Boy," the thin clerk said. "Boy — what — what *is* that?"

Lu Yan let the maul dissolve. He let the brazier fade back into the red place
inside him where it lived. He let his eyes, which had gone the banked-ember color of
the crucible's heart, cool back to plain frontier brown. And he gave the examiner
the answer he had already decided to give, in the flat voice of a child who has
learned that the wrong word in a place like Slagford could get you sold.

"I don't know," Lu Yan said. "The crystal did it. I didn't do anything."

It was the first lie of his new life. It was a good one. He watched it work — watched
the clerk *want* to believe it, watched fear go looking for a story it could live
with — and he understood that lying, too, would be easy, because nothing in him
flinched from it, and the part of him that had once been a mild man on a train
filed that away with all the rest.

The examiner wrote for a long time that morning. That night, after the children were
sent in and the yard was empty and the dead grass had begun, already, to be covered
by the first true snow, the clerk wrote a second time — a shorter letter, sealed
twice, addressed not to the prefecture but east of it, much further east, to a white
tower in a city Lu Yan had only read about, where an institution that bought healers
and made gods kept careful records of every anomaly the wide world coughed up.

*Western frontier,* the letter said, in part. *Slagford. Anomalous awakening.
Unclassifiable martial soul, presenting tool- and beast-traits both. Spirit color:
red. Repeat: red, at awakening, in a six-year-old. Recommend observation. Recommend
caution. Recommend, if the subject proves what the color suggests, that he be
acquired before he understands what he is — or removed before anyone else does.*

The letter went east on the next caravan, into the snow, toward the white tower.

In the tallhouse loft, in the dark, Lu Yan lay among the breathing of the other
children and did not sleep. He was warm — still warm, faintly, from the rat — and
the warmth was a lie and he knew it was a lie and he held onto it anyway, the way a
cold man holds onto any fire, and he stared up into the dark and began, methodically,
the way he did everything, to plan.

He had a whole ladder to climb. He had a furnace that would carry him up it. And he
had, at last, an answer to the worry he had carried since the crib — the worry that
something in him was already missing.

Something *was* missing. It had been missing since before he could name it. But for
the first time in two lives, Lu Yan had found a power that did not need him to be
whole.

It only needed him to be willing.

He found, examining it in the dark with his usual thoroughness, that he was.

That, more than the red crystal or the dead grass or the letter going east through
the snow, was the thing that should have frightened the world.

It would. In time.

But the frontier is patient, and the snow falls early in the west, and on the first
night of the rest of his long and terrible climb, the only one awake to be afraid was
a six-year-old boy who felt nothing at all, and planned.
