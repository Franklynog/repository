# Chapter 2 — Slag and Cinder

*Lu Yan, age 6 · Spirit Apprentice (rank 11, concealed) · 0 rings · POV: Lu Yan · Slagford*

---

Power, Lu Yan decided that winter, was a forge, and like a forge it was useless
without three things: fuel, a vessel to hold the heat, and a smith who knew what he
intended to make. He had the fuel — the whole world was fuel, that was the horror and
the gift of the crucible. He had the vessel, growing slowly, one ungovernable child's
year at a time. What he did not have, and went looking for with the patience of a man
who has already died once and learned that nothing is owed to anyone, was the smith.

He found him at the bottom of Slagford, which was where Slagford kept the things it
had used up.

---

The forge sat below the slag-heaps, where the town's runoff went and its broken
things went and, by long unspoken agreement, its broken people went too. It was a
low building of soot-black stone with a chimney that had not drawn properly in a
decade, and it belonged to a man the town called Old Cinder, though that was not his
name, and treated with the particular contempt reserved for people who were once
something and now were not.

Lu Yan had a use for a man like that. Used-up people, he had observed in two lives,
told the truth more often than whole ones. They had nothing left to protect.

He went down to the forge on the third morning after his awakening, while the snow
held off and the town still flinched when he passed, and he stood in the doorway and
watched the old man work until the old man got tired of being watched.

Old Cinder was big the way a fallen wall is big — a ruin of what had clearly once
been an enormous frame, now bent and listing, the left side of him dragging when he
moved as if the strings on that half had been cut. One eye was milky. The hand on the
dead side curled permanently half-shut, and he did everything one-handed and
furious, hammering a cart-axle straight with a precision that did not match the
slovenliness of the rest of him. He did not look up.

"Charity cot's at the tallhouse, boy," he said. "Not down here. Down here you sweep
or you bleed."

"I'll sweep," Lu Yan said.

The hammer paused. The milky eye and the good one both came up and looked at him, and
something moved behind the good one — recognition, of a kind. The whole town had
heard about the red crystal by now. Frontier news traveled on fear, and fear was
fast.

"You're the furnace child," Old Cinder said.

"I'm the boy who'll sweep your floor for a place by your fire," Lu Yan said. "What I
am besides that is my business."

The old man looked at him a while longer. Then, unexpectedly, he barked a single
laugh — a rusty sound, like a hinge that had forgotten the motion — and jerked his
chin at the broom in the corner, and went back to his axle, and that was the whole of
the negotiation. Lu Yan swept. He was good at it; he was good at anything that was
mostly patience. And while he swept he watched the old man's one good hand, and the
fire, and the iron, and he began the long slow work of learning a thing his crucible
had given him an appetite for and no instruction in.

Because that was the discovery of his first winter, and it surprised even him.

The Crimson Crucible was not only a mouth. He had understood, that first red morning,
that it could take — could drink a life and turn it to warmth, could open its
channels and let the world pay the cost. He had assumed, in the cold arithmetic of
his planning, that taking was all it did. A furnace that ate. A ladder that climbed
on other people's lives.

He had been wrong, and the wrongness was the first interesting thing that had
happened to him in either life.

The crucible could *make.*

He felt it the first time he watched Old Cinder ruin a weld — the old man's dead hand
slipping, the heat held a half-breath too long, the iron going from orange to white
to a spitting, crumbling grey at the join. Waste. A flaw. And something in Lu Yan,
the same something that had reached out and pinned the rat, *itched* at it — itched
the way a word on the tip of the tongue itches — because he could feel, standing six
feet away with a broom in his hands, exactly where the heat had gone wrong and
exactly how the crucible inside him would have held it. Not hotter. *Cleaner.* The
furnace at his back did not merely burn. It *refined.* It took the flawed and the
mixed and the wasted and it knew, the way it knew how to drink, how to drive the flaw
out and leave the metal true.

He did not tell the old man this. He filed it, the way he filed everything, and he
swept, and he watched, and he learned the human craft from the outside while the
inhuman one waited in him like a held breath. He was patient. He had decided, in the
crib, that patience was the only thing his strange flat soul was truly built for, and
he was determined to make it a weapon.

"You watch the iron like you want to eat it," Old Cinder said one evening, a month
in, not turning around.

"I want to understand it," Lu Yan said.

"Same thing, with some men." The old man set down his hammer. The good eye found him
across the red light of the banked forge, and for once there was no contempt in it,
only a tired and terrible kind of knowing. "I had a soul once, furnace child. Did
they tell you? Down here they tell everyone, it's the only story I'm good for now."

"They told me you were a soldier."

"They told you I was a soldier." Old Cinder lifted his ruined left hand, the curled
fingers, the dead side of his face. "A soldier is what's left when the soul master
loses. I had a hammer-soul. A good one — a war-hammer, a real one, not a smith's
toy. Rank fifty-three, in my day. A Spirit King." He said the title the way other men
said the names of the dead. "I marched east in the border war and I met a man with a
soul stronger than mine, and he didn't kill me, which I have hated him for every day
since, because killing me would have been a kindness. He *broke* it. Broke the bond.
You can do that, to a soul, if you're strong enough and cruel enough and you want the
other man to live with it." He flexed the dead hand; it did not move. "Now I've got a
broom-boy's idea of a soul. A coal that won't catch. I can feel my hammer in here
some nights, furnace child, the way you feel a leg they've cut off. And I can't lift
it. That's the war. That's all the war ever is, when you get under the flags. One man
takes a thing from another man that can't ever be given back."

Lu Yan was quiet for a moment. Not from feeling — he searched himself and found, as
always, the cool flat nothing where the feeling should be — but because the old man
had just handed him something more valuable than sympathy, which was *information*,
and he had learned that the way to keep information flowing was to let a man think
his pain had landed somewhere soft.

"Does it still hurt," he asked. "The hammer you can't lift."

"Every day," Old Cinder said. "That's how you know it was real."

Lu Yan filed it. *A soul can be broken by another's will, not only killed. Note: the
bond is the vulnerability. Note: cruelty as policy — leave the enemy alive and
ruined. The Tallyman? Spirit Hall?* And beneath the cold cabinet of his planning,
quieter, in the place he did not look: *the old man's pain is how he knows the hammer
was real. The dead grass is how I'll know the same.* He did not let himself finish the
thought. He had years of not finishing it ahead of him.

"Teach me the forge," he said instead. "The real way. Not the broom."

"Why," said Old Cinder. "So you can fix axles in the bottom of the world?"

"So that when I leave this place," Lu Yan said, "I'll know how a thing is made before
I decide whether to make it or break it."

The old man looked at him for a long, long moment in the red dark. Whatever he saw —
and he had been a Spirit King once, and a Spirit King who has lost everything sees
clearly, having nothing left to fool himself for — he did not name it. He only nodded,
slowly, the way a man nods at weather he can't change.

"You're a cold little thing, furnace child," he said. "Coldest I've met, and I've met
the man who broke my soul." He picked the hammer back up, and turned to the fire, and
the good eye glinted. "Good. Cold iron takes the truest edge. Pick up the small
hammer. Mind your hand. The forge doesn't care that you're six, and neither, I am
beginning to suspect, do you."

---

The girl was a different problem, and Lu Yan circled her for the whole of that winter
the way he circled everything he did not yet understand: slowly, from a distance,
taking notes.

Mei Sang did not fear him.

This was so anomalous that he assumed at first he had misread it. The whole town
feared him — a soft, useful fear, the kind that left him room, the kind he was
carefully cultivating by being unfailingly quiet and unfailingly strange. The other
orphans had stopped sharing his corner of the loft. Even Ke Lang, who feared nothing
he could hit, had taken to watching Lu Yan with a wariness that was its own kind of
respect. Fear was the medium Lu Yan had decided to swim in. It was clean. It asked
nothing of him he did not have.

Mei Sang was not afraid of him, and she would not stop talking to him, and she kept
*giving him things.*

A heel of bread, once, when his portion had been short. A strip of rag for the
burn-scar he took on his left forearm the first time the forge taught him a lesson
about held heat. A place by her on the loft's one warm beam, scooting over without
being asked, as if it were nothing, as if he were a person and not a furnace that had
learned to walk. She had a healer's soul and a healer's instinct, the prefecture
clerk had written *modest* in his book and the clerk was a fool, because the thing
Mei Sang did was not modest at all. She looked at broken things and wanted to mend
them. She looked at *him* — at the cold flat strange wrongness of him that he had
spent two lives learning to hide and had stopped bothering to hide out here at the
end of the world — and instead of recoiling she had simply, quietly, decided he was
broken, and that broken things were hers to tend.

"You don't have to do that," he told her, the third or fourth time she gave him
something. The bread, that time. He had not asked for it. He never asked for anything;
asking was a debt.

"I know," Mei Sang said.

"Then why."

She looked at him with the green-flecked eyes that her soul had colored, and she
thought about it seriously, the way she thought about everything, and she said: "When
the sparrow died, you watched me cry and you didn't feel anything. I saw you not feel
it. Everybody else looked away because it made them sad. You looked *at* me because it
was interesting." She said it without accusation, the way you'd report the weather.
"I figured anybody who can't feel things should at least have somebody around who can,
so they remember what it's supposed to look like. Otherwise you'll forget there's a
shape to it. And then you'll be like the Tallyman's men." She handed him the bread,
final, as if the matter were closed. "Eat. You're growing. Cold things need more
fuel."

Lu Yan took the bread.

He told himself it was efficient to take it. He told himself that a person who
insisted on giving him things was an asset, that her healing soul would be useful,
that the bond she was building was a leash she was putting on herself and a fool gives
away leashes. He told himself all of it, fluently, in the cold cabinet of his mind,
and all of it was true, and none of it was why he took the bread, and the gap between
the reason he gave himself and the reason that actually moved his hand was the first
crack in the flat grey wall of him that he had ever noticed from the inside.

He did not look at the crack. He had years of not looking at it ahead of him.

But he noted, with his usual thoroughness, that Mei Sang had said the Tallyman's men
as if they were a known and present danger, and that her clear eyes had gone, for
just a moment, to the east road when she said it.

"Who is the Tallyman," he said.

Mei Sang's whole small body did the thing he had watched it do over the dead
sparrow — folded, briefly, around something heavier than she was. Then she
straightened, because she was tough, because everything out here was tough or dead.

"You'll find out in the spring," she said. "When the tithe comes due. Everybody finds
out then." She pulled her knees up under her chin and looked at the east road, and
her voice when it came again was very quiet and very flat, the flattest he had ever
heard it, almost as flat as his own.

"He counts," she said. "That's all he does. He counts what the frontier's worth and
he takes the difference. Money, when there's money. Beasts, when there's beasts.
And every spring, children." She did not look away from the road. "Awakened ones.
The strong ones. He has a soul that lets him *know* — lets him look at a child and
read what they'll be worth, like reading a tally-stick. And he takes the ones that
read high, and he sells them east, to the white tower, and nobody ever sees them
again, and the prefecture lets him because the prefecture gets a cut and the white
tower gets its soul masters and the only ones who lose are us, who are too far west
to matter." She finally looked at Lu Yan, and there was something in her face that
was almost an apology, almost a warning, exactly a fear — but not a fear for herself.

"Your crystal went red, Lu Yan," Mei Sang said softly. "The whole town saw it. The
whole town's been afraid of you for a season. But fear's slow, out here. The
Tallyman's not." She wrapped her arms tighter around her knees. "When he comes in
the spring and reads you — and he'll read you, he reads everyone — what do you think
a tally-stick says, about a six-year-old whose crystal went *red?*"

Lu Yan sat very still in the cold loft with the heel of bread in his hand, and for
the second time in his second life, he did the arithmetic, and felt the whole shape
of an answer arrive.

Somewhere east, a letter was already traveling toward a white tower, carrying his
description in a clerk's careful hand. *Acquire him before he understands what he is,*
the letter said, though Lu Yan did not yet know its exact words — *or remove him.*
And here, west, closer, faster, a man with a counting soul was going to walk into
Slagford in the spring and look at him and read, on whatever tally-stick the world
kept, a number large enough to be worth a great deal of money to a great many
dangerous people.

He had planned for the long climb. He had planned for years of patience, of
concealment, of taking the ladder one careful rung at a time while his body grew up
around the furnace at his core.

He had not planned for the spring to be a deadline.

The arithmetic finished. It gave him an answer he did not love and could not argue
with, the way his arithmetic always did, and he set the bread down half-eaten — the
first time in two lives he had ever left food uneaten, and Mei Sang noticed, and her
eyes widened a fraction — and he looked out at the east road and the west wildlands
both, and he understood that the patient plan was dead, and a faster, harder, far
more dangerous plan would have to be born to replace it before the snow melted.

To be worth too much to the Tallyman was to be cargo.

To be worth nothing to him was to be invisible.

And to be worth *more than he could carry* — to be a thing the Tallyman's counting
soul looked at and read not as profit but as *threat*, as a number so large and so
red that the only safe move was to leave it alone or die trying to take it —

— that was a thing a furnace child could become, before the spring, if the furnace
child was willing to go west into the Ashwood and pay the kind of price the crucible
charged.

Which was to say: if he was willing to let something else pay it.

"Mei Sang," Lu Yan said, in the cold dark, in his flat quiet voice. "Where does the
caravan road meet the Ashwood. The old way, the one nobody uses."

She turned to look at him, and whatever she saw in his banked-ember stillness made the
breath catch in her throat — not fear of him, never quite that, but fear *for* him,
and something underneath the fear that he did not have a name for and would not learn
the name of for years.

"Lu Yan," she said. "The Ashwood kills grown soul masters. You're *six.*"

"I know how old I am," Lu Yan said. "Where does the road meet the wood."

And Mei Sang, who could not stop tending broken things even when the broken thing was
walking knowingly toward the dark — Mei Sang looked at him for a long, long moment in
the red glow of the distant forge, and then she told him, because she had decided long
ago that the alternative to going with him into the things he could not feel was
leaving him to face them alone, and she would not do that, not for the coldest boy on
the frontier, not for anyone.

"I'll show you," she said. "But I'm coming. You'll need somebody who can feel it when
you're about to die, since clearly you won't feel it yourself."

It was, Lu Yan reflected, an extremely inefficient arrangement.

He agreed to it anyway, and did not look too closely at why, and added the not-looking
to the growing list of things in himself he had decided to leave for later.

Outside, the first true thaw-wind of the year came down off the western wildlands,
and somewhere under the snow, the dead circle of grass in the tallhouse yard waited
for a spring it would not, this year, be part of.
