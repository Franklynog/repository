Chapter 1 — The Sixth Birthday

Volume 1: Hunter · Huo Yuan, age 6 · Lieshan Village, Year 6

Eleven arrows. Three with good fletching, six passable, two that would tumble before twenty paces. Huo Yuan laid them out on the doorstep in the grey light before dawn and counted them twice, because counting them once had never felt like enough.

"You'll wear the feathers out with looking," his father said from inside the house.

"The grey one's crooked."

"Then it'll fly crooked, and you'll learn to aim crooked." Huo Tieshan came out into the doorway, ducking under the lintel the way big men learned to duck without thinking. He had his good boots on, the ones he only wore for festival days and funerals, and he'd oiled them until they shone like wet stones. "Put them away. No hunting today."

"It's only a ceremony."

"It's only your sixth birthday, and it's only the day the whole village finds out what you are." His father took the arrows from him one at a time, in no hurry, and set them in the quiver by the door. His hands were the largest things Yuan knew, scarred across the knuckles, missing the top joint of the left ring finger. They were never rough with him. "Wash your neck. Your mother would skin me."

His mother had been dead for two years, but in their house she was still the rule everything was measured against. Yuan washed his neck.

The shrine hill stood at the east end of Lieshan, forty households' worth of village gathered at its foot like sheep at a gate. The shrine itself was old past anyone's remembering — a squat stone room with a swaybacked roof, built around a slab of darker rock that the grandmothers said had fallen from the sky and the grandfathers said was just a rock. Once a year, in spring, the Spirit Hall examiner came up the forest road on a mule, and every child who had turned six that year climbed the hill, and the village learned which of its children were people of consequence.

This year there were five of them. Yuan stood last in line, because the line went by birth date and he had been born in the last month of winter, and he passed the waiting the only way he knew how. Fifty-one villagers on the hill, not counting children under six. Four hens that had followed the crowd up. One examiner.

The examiner was a young man in white and yellow robes that had been splendid for somebody else first — the hem was taken up with thread that didn't match. He had set a folding table in front of the shrine, and on the table sat the crystal: a milky sphere on a brass stand, the size of a goose egg. Yuan had seen it once a year his whole life and never up close.

"By grace of Spirit Hall," the examiner recited, in the voice of a man who had said it in nine villages already that week, "all are born with a soul, and at six years the soul wakes. Approach when your name is called. Place your hand upon the crystal. Do not be afraid. The crystal does not judge." He paused at the end the way people pause when they've made a joke for themselves. Of course it judged. Judging was the whole of it.

Wei Dahu went first, the miller's son, big for six. His martial soul came up out of him in a shimmer of yellow light and turned out to be a millstone. The crowd made a kind sound. The examiner made a note. "Tool soul. Millstone. Soul power, level one." A farmer soul, then, like his father — a life of grinding, no rings, no rank, no road out of Lieshan. Wei Dahu's mother hugged him anyway, hard enough to lift his feet off the ground, and Yuan watched that and filed it away without a word for what it was he was feeling.

Second, the herbalist's daughter, a sprig of bitterleaf — useful, the examiner allowed, in a small way. Third, a hoe. The crowd's kindness was wearing thin by the hoe; somebody near the back laughed and was elbowed quiet.

Fourth, Liu Yan, the headman's granddaughter, put her hand on the crystal and the air went cold and bright, and a hawk made of pale light spread its wings above her, every feather distinct as cut paper.

"Beast soul," the examiner said, sitting up. "Wind hawk. Soul power—" The crystal had gone a clear, ringing blue. "Level four. Innate full soul power." He was writing quickly now. "The girl is eligible for enrollment at an academy of the Hall's designation. Spirit Hall will bear the cost."

The hill went up like a festival. Liu Yan's grandfather wept into his beard. Somewhere in the noise the hens panicked and were chased. It took the examiner a while to restore the line, and by the time he did, the sun was properly up and warm and there was only one name left.

"Huo Yuan."

Walking forward took eight steps. He counted them. The crystal up close had a flaw in its heart, a little frozen feather of white, and the brass stand was green at the seams. The examiner smelled of mule and chalk dust. None of this is important, his father would say, when you sight a deer you see the deer, not the leaves — but Yuan's eyes had always grabbed everything and let go of nothing, and they did it now while his hand went out and settled on the crystal, which was cold.

For the space of a breath, nothing. Then the morning dimmed.

It dimmed the way it dims when a cloud crosses the sun, except there was no cloud, and the dimming had a shape. The warmth went off the back of Yuan's neck, and the hill went quiet in two waves — first the front rows, then the back — and the examiner, looking up from his ledger at whatever now stood behind the boy, rose halfway out of his chair without seeming to notice he'd done it.

Yuan turned his head and looked up at his own soul.

It stood the height of a door and a half. It was a figure in robes, and the robes were red — not festival red, not blood fresh from the vein, but the deep tired red of a wound gone old, of embers at the bottom of a banked fire — and they hung from shoulder to ground in folds that did not move with the wind, because the wind went around them. Where a face should have been, the hood held only a dark like the inside of a closed hand. It carried nothing. It reached for nothing. Its hands — gauntleted, or perhaps the gauntlets were the hands — were folded before it in the manner of a priest at a graveside, and it stood at Yuan's back the way his father stood behind him when he drew his first bow: close, still, and entirely certain of its right to be there.

There was a smell, faint, that Yuan would spend years finding words for: cold incense and hot iron. Ash, and under the ash, the memory of burning.

One of the hens had come back up the hill. It took a single look and went down again.

"Beast soul," the examiner said, too fast, to his ledger. Then, looking up, slower: "No. Tool soul? It's — " He came around the table. He walked a full circle around Yuan and the figure, at a radius Yuan noted was the radius of a careful man, and the figure did not turn to track him, and somehow that was worse, and the examiner's circle got wider on the second pass. "An idol," he said at last. "An idol soul. A figure of — some god or other." He laughed, a short startled sound with nothing in it. "Of what, heaven knows. There's no scripture in red. Boy — does it speak? Does it move?"

"I don't know, honored sir," Yuan said. "It's new."

A grandmother in the third row made the sign against omens, both hands. He saw it. He saw, in one slow sweep of the crowd that he had not meant to make and could not stop, all of it: the herbalist drawing her daughter back by the shoulder, the headman's mouth gone flat, Wei Dahu staring with his mouth open, fifty-one people in the morning sun and not one of them standing quite as near as they had stood a minute ago. Only his father had not moved. His father had his arms folded and his head tipped a little to one side, studying the figure the way he studied a new dog, and when Yuan's eyes found him he looked back steadily and did not look away, which was a thing Yuan kept.

"The crystal, boy. The reading." The examiner was back behind his table, voice recovered, pen up. "Hand flat."

The crystal sat under Yuan's hand, milky and dumb, the little feather-flaw unchanged. No blue. No color at all. The examiner frowned, shook the brass stand, polished the sphere on his sleeve as if the fault might be smudge, and set Yuan's hand back on it, pressing it flat with his own, as if more skin might help.

"Level zero," he said at last, and the silence behind him got a texture to it, like wet wool. "No soul power. None." He looked from the crystal to the towering, patient, wound-red figure and back, and what crossed his face was the discomfort of a clerk asked to file a thing for which the forms have no box. "It happens," he said, finding the road back to his script and walking it with relief. "Rarely. One in ten thousand — a manifestation with no power behind it. A martial soul with no soul power is a picture of a feast, boy; it'll feed you what a picture feeds you. No power, no rings. No rings, no rank." He wrote, in characters Yuan was too far away to read and would spend years of his life imagining: idol-type tool soul, unidentified; soul power zero. "He's healthy otherwise. He can farm."

When Yuan lifted his hand from the crystal, the figure went out — not fading, not sinking, but all at once, between blink and blink, the way a candle goes when the snuffer comes down. The sun arrived back on the hill. It found nobody standing within five paces of the hunter's son.

He can farm. On the way down the hill Yuan heard it travel through the crowd ahead of him, changing shape as it went, and the shapes were not the shapes of pity. Did you see its hands. A funeral god, my mother knew the look of them. No power, the glass said — yes, but did you SEE it. The hunter's boy. The one whose mother died. Ghost-priest. Ill-starred. Wei Dahu's mother gave him a pancake folded in a leaf at the foot of the hill, the way you'd give one to a beggar at the door — out of a goodness that had pity baked all through it, and under the pity, fear, because she gave it at arm's length and her eyes went once, quickly, over his shoulder, to the empty air where the figure had stood. He thanked her politely, because his mother was a rule and the rule held, and he carried the pancake at his side all the way home without eating any of it.

His father said nothing until they were inside with the door shut. Then he crouched, which brought their eyes level, and took Yuan's shoulders in those two huge hands.

"Did the glass hurt you?"

"No."

"Did the tall fellow?"

"No." And then, because the question had loosened something: "They're afraid of it."

"They're afraid of everything they can't put in a granary. It's how villages last." His father's eyes were dark amber, like his own; in the dim of the house they were nearly black, and they were not afraid, and they were not lying about it. "I got the longest look at him of anyone on that hill except you. You know what I saw? He stands straight. Soldier's posture. Hands quiet, feet set — he stood your ground, not his. And the glass—" He turned his head and spat, neatly, into the hearth, which Yuan had never once seen him do indoors. "The glass is a tool other men use to weigh other men. We're not in that trade. A hunter's worth isn't read from glass — it's read from the snow, in the morning, by what's still moving and what fed whom. You hear me?"

"Yes," Yuan said, and meant it, and didn't believe it, both at once, the way you can at six.

His father studied him a moment longer. "Tomorrow we'll cut you a real bow," he said. "Birch. You're long enough in the arm now." Which was how Yuan knew his father had been saving the birch stave behind the rafters for the day the crystal sang for him, and had decided in the space of one afternoon that the bow would not wait on any glass, or on any priest either. The roaring in Yuan's ears eased. They ate the pancake between them, halved exactly.

He woke in the dark because the window was red.

Not fire-red, not the leaping light of a burning byre that every forest-edge child is weaned on fear of. This was steady. A clean, deep red lay along the windowsill like spilled dye — the exact red, he understood at once and without surprise, of the robes that had stood behind him on the hill — and when Yuan slid out from under the quilt and looked out, he found the color came from above: a star over the shrine hill, low and large and wrong. He knew the night sky the way he knew his own arrows; his father had taught him the hunter's stars before he could read. There was no star there. There had never been a star there.

It did not occur to him to wake his father, and that — he understood much later — was not courage and not foolishness but a draft from far inside, a door open somewhere, drawing; the same somewhere the figure had come out of. He put on his boots. He went up the hill in his nightshirt with the dew soaking his ankles, and the village slept, every window dark, as if the red light fell on him alone. Fifty-one people on this hill this morning, he thought, and one tonight.

Under the star, the shrine was changed. The old slab — the grandmothers' sky-stone — had split. Not shattered: opened, along a seam no one had ever found because until tonight it had not existed, and in the seam something glowed the same red as the star, the same red as the robes, breathing slow, like a coal at the bottom of a banked fire.

Yuan stood in the doorway for the space of forty heartbeats. He counted them. Some part of him was already keeping the ledger that would one day have armies in it, and the entry it made that night was honest: I could have gone home. Nothing made me. I wanted to see.

He reached into the seam.

What lay in the stone was a sliver of metal the length of his finger, red-black, neither hot nor cold, heavier than anything that small had a right to be — and the moment his skin touched it, it moved. It went through his palm the way a fish goes through a gap in a weir, smooth and total and gone, and the pain arrived afterward, a crescent of white fire across his hand, and the world went away.

A battlefield, seen from above. Banners burning. A red sky and an army kneeling in rows that ran to the horizon, and at the head of it a figure in robes the color of a wound — the figure, his figure, the one from the hill, but vast now, holding up one hand against a darkness that was eating the stars — and then the figure breaking, the way the slab had broken, along seams of light, into nine falling embers. One ember fell forever, and Yuan fell with it, and a voice spoke. Not in his ears. In his bones, the way you hear your own pulse when you've run too far.

It did not speak in words, quite, but the meaning pressed itself into him and took shapes from what a six-year-old had: the examiner's grading voice, his father's trade, the village's hunger for rank.

An heir is registered. The Vestige stood for you at your waking; now the path stands under it.

What your glass-priests saw is the shadow of a dead god's seat, cast through your flesh. It is not your power. It is the shape your power will fill, name by name, if you climb.

Class: Hunter. The ninth Sequence of the Crimson Path.

Order: physical. The flesh is the temple and the weapon; the figure is the temple's shadow. No glass shall weigh it, no hall shall hold it.

Grade: S — the highest. Sufficient, alone among the living, to climb.

Hunt. Act the role until the role is true. The path ascends through nine names, and at the summit the Vestige is no shadow, and the chair beneath it is empty.

The price of climbing wrongly is the Tide. The price of stopping is being eaten. The price of the chair is everything.

Begin, Hunter.

The red star went out.

He came to himself on the shrine floor with dawn coming grey through the doorway and dew gone to chill in his nightshirt, and his first thought was made of his father's training and nothing else: how long, and what saw me while I lay here. The slab was whole. He put his hands on the seam and there was no seam; the stone was one stone, as it had been for nine hundred years, and it would all have looked like a dream entire — except.

Except that the morning came in sharp as a knife-edge. He could hear, distinctly, three separate birds in the oaks down the hill and the creek beyond them and a vole moving under the shrine floor. He could smell the cold ash of the incense burner and under it the mule, yesterday's mule, a scent gone twelve hours that hung in the room plain as smoke. His body felt like the new bow his father had promised: cut down to the true grain, strung, quiet. When he stood, he stood in one motion, without putting a hand down, and the strangeness of that frightened him more than the voice had.

And except for his hand.

Across his right palm, where the sliver had gone in, lay a scar like a crescent moon no bigger than a thumbnail, white-edged, pink-new, and warm. As he stared at it, it itched once, briefly, in the direction of the forest — in a direction, the way no itch he'd ever had came with a compass in it — and then lay still.

He tried, once, standing in the cold doorway with the village waking below, to call the figure the way he'd heard ring-bearing soul masters call their souls — and felt, in answer, not the figure but the door again, the deep inward dark it stood in, patient as winter. Not yet, the dark didn't say, in no words at all. Climb.

He told no one. He walked home through a village just waking, fetched the water, fed the fire, and sat down across from his father, who looked at him a beat too long over the rim of his tea bowl but asked nothing, because Huo Tieshan was a man who let game come to the blind in its own time. They spent the morning on the hill pasture cutting the birch stave, and Yuan drew it before noon — a thing that should have taken a boy of six both arms and a knee and still failed — drew it smooth to his ear, loosed, and put the arrow through the heart of the straw boar at thirty paces.

His father stood very still.

"Again," he said.

Yuan put the second arrow beside the first, close enough that the shafts touched. The crooked grey one. It flew true; somehow he'd known what its crookedness would do, the way he knew where his own fingers were.

His father came over, knelt, and took Yuan's right hand, turning it palm up — not the bow hand; he had seen something all the same, because he had spent thirty years reading the morning snow. He looked for a long time at the small white crescent.

"That wasn't there at the festival," he said.

"No."

"Does it hurt?"

"No." And then, because his father had not looked away from him on the hill in front of fifty-one people, Yuan gave him what he had: "There was a red star last night. I went up. Something — went in. There was a voice. It said the tall fellow is a shadow. The shadow of a dead god's chair, and the chair has a path up to it, and the path has nine names, and I wear the ninth. It said—" The words came back to him strange in his own mouth, a grading no crystal would ever make. "It said: class, Hunter. Rank S. The highest. It said begin."

He watched his father do something he had never seen him do: turn and look at the forest line, all of it, slow, the way he checked the treeline when a sow had gone in wounded, as if the trees had been listening.

"We'll say nothing," Huo Tieshan said at last. "Not to the headman, not at the well. The village will talk about the priest for a season — let them. Talk of a powerless freak soul is a scarecrow; it stands in the field and frightens nothing worth frightening. But the star, the stone, the voice, the hand — that's nobody's. The Hall graded you a zero yesterday, and a zero is a door no one guards." He squeezed Yuan's hand once, around the scar, swallowing it whole in his grip. "Hm. But there's one man I want to see that hand. He's safe, and he's old, and he knows things a village tanner has no business knowing."

They went down to the tannery by the creek that evening, into the eye-watering reek of oak-bark liquor, where Old Ge sat mending a hide with an awl, his ruined leg propped on a stool. The old man heard them out of the dusk and spoke before they reached the fence, without looking up, in the mild voice of village gossip:

"Well's been busy. They say the Huo boy called up a god with no face and the glass called it worthless." The awl went through the hide, drew the waxed thread after. "They say the robes were red. Festival red, says the miller. Blood red, says his wife." The awl stopped. "Nobody's said it right, have they. It was the red of a wound gone old."

Yuan's father went very still beside him. "You weren't on the hill, Ge."

"No," the old man said. "I wasn't." He set the hide down and held out one bark-stained hand, palm up, and his eyes in the lamplight were not a tanner's eyes at all. "Show me the boy's hand."

He took Yuan's hand the way he took a hide, flat and businesslike, and tilted it to the lamp, and Yuan — who was already, without knowing it, becoming the thing the voice had named him — watched the blood leave the old man's face the way warmth leaves a body: from the outside in.

"Ge?" his father said.

Old Ge looked up. He looked at Huo Tieshan, and then for a long time at Yuan, and longest of all at the empty air over the boy's shoulder, where fifty-one people had learned the shape of a faceless priest.

"Close the door," he said.
