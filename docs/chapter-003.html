Chapter 3 — Smoke on the Treeline

Volume 1: Hunter · Huo Yuan, age 7 · Lieshan Village, Year 7

The itch began on the third day of the sixth month, and it pointed up the road.

Yuan was seven by then, and the crescent scar had been quiet so long it had become like the count: a thing he checked nightly out of discipline, expecting nothing. He was stacking bark when his palm prickled — faint, the way it prickled toward deep forest when something old moved there — except this time it lay along the line of the west road like a needle on a wet leaf, and it did not stop.

He told Old Ge with one look. They had built the look that winter, between hides.

"How strong?" the old man said, not pausing his awl.

"Like a flea bite."

"Then they're days out yet. It'll climb to a wasp sting, and then to a burn. Tell your father: the burn means hours." Old Ge set the awl down with terrible gentleness. "And the third part falls due, cub. From tonight, both of you sleep in your boots."

The third part, as the old man had finally laid it out, was simple the way a snare is simple. When the burn came, Yuan and his father would go — out the back pasture, over the creek at the dry stones, to the blind two valleys deep that they had spent the spring stocking, and they would not stop, look back, or come back, for three days. The village would be left to answer honestly, because the village honestly knew nothing: a zero-rated boy had apprenticed to the tanner, the shrine was a stone, and nobody in Lieshan had dreamed red.

"And you?" Yuan's father had asked.

"I'm an old cripple who'll be sitting in plain sight, stinking of bark, telling them about my leg," Old Ge said. "A man with one secret can hide it. A village can't hide a missing tanner. The hounds come to the shrine first — there's nine hundred years of the shard's sleep soaked into that hill, and it'll hold their noses a while. The trail out of the shrine is a year old and ends in a boy the glass itself marked empty. They'll sniff, they'll press, they'll find a cold nest, and grey men don't waste a month on a cold nest when the thing they want is begun and moving somewhere in the world. The one mistake — the only mistake — would be giving them something warm."

It was a good plan. Yuan wrote it in the book that night, all of it, and under it the count of what they'd cached at the blind, and the entry steadied him the way the entries always did.

The plan had one flaw in it, and the flaw was the kind of man his father was.

The sting became a burn at dusk on the eighth day, between one breath and the next — his whole palm, like the candle held too long — and Yuan said "Now," and his father took down the boar spear and the war bow from the rafters without a word, and they went out the back as the light went copper, two shadows along a hedge they'd practiced until the practicing was boring.

From the pasture gate, Lieshan looked the way it had looked on a thousand evenings. Supper smoke standing up straight from forty chimneys. The creek saying the one thing it knew. Swallows. His father paused there, only a heartbeat, the boar spear over his shoulder — and Yuan would keep that picture all his life, because that was the last moment the world was whole: his father in the gate, the village laid out below in the copper light, swallows going over, everything counted and everything still.

Then the screaming started by the well, and a barn went up in flame with a sound like a struck drum, and smoke rose on the treeline where smoke had no business — and the picture broke.

There were six of them, after, when Yuan assembled the count from what he had seen and what the village told and what the dead themselves had to say about it. Five men in grey — ash-grey, road-grey, the grey of things burned out — and a sixth shape that went on four legs and was not a dog, though it had started as one. They had come up the west road at supper-time, polite as priests, and asked the headman the question, and the headman, who was brave and stupid in the way of small authority, had lied to them badly. So they had put his arm wrong at the elbow, and fired the Wei barn to light the square, and gathered the village to the well to ask everyone else.

Yuan saw most of it from the smoke-house ridge, flat in the dock weeds, because his father had stopped at the pasture gate and looked down at the burning, and Yuan had seen the plan die in his face.

"Father. The third part. We don't stop, we don't look back—"

"Listen to me." His father crouched, eye level, the way he did when a thing mattered; the firelight was in his face and his voice was the one he used to settle frightened animals, which was how Yuan knew. "The plan holds for you. It was always for you; Ge knows it, I know it, you're old enough to know it tonight. Those people are at the well because of what's sleeping in your hand. That doesn't make it your debt — you were six, you touched a stone — but it makes it somebody's, and of the two of us, I'm the one a hunting party can afford to meet." He took the war bow off his shoulder and strung it, his thumb finding the nock-point worn pale with years. "Go to the ford. Ge's third part. Whatever he tells you — do it, and don't ask why. That's my last order, soldier."

"It's not your debt either," Yuan said. His voice came out very small.

His father smiled at that, the swift rare one. "No," he said. "It's my village." And he was gone down the hedge-line into the dark with no more sound than a draft makes, and Yuan — who would obey the order, who was already turning toward the ford, who would tell himself for years that he had been turning toward the ford — went flat in the dock weeds on the ridge instead, because he was seven, and the man going down the hedge was everything he had.

What he watched from the ridge took less time than the watching of it.

The first arrow came out of the dark beyond the well and took the man holding the headman through the chest, full draw at sixty paces, and the second had killed a torch-bearer before the first finished falling — one, two, Yuan's mind wrote in the book it kept now without being asked. The square boiled. Villagers fled between the houses; grey men moved through them with a horrible professional calm, fanning out toward the dark with hook-bladed chains coming off their belts; and the dark answered with a third arrow that struck the leader and turned on a plate beneath the grey robe and stood in his shoulder instead of his heart.

The leader took the arrow's bearing with his arm still through the headman's, like a man noting the wind. He said something. The four-legged shape went out of the square, low and wrong-jointed and silent, swinging wide of the archer's line — and a part of Yuan that was being born that very hour, cold and counting, understood the shape had been sent to find what the arrows were protecting, and that it was casting for a scent it already half knew, and that the scent was him.

The rest of him watched his father come into the firelight, because the bow was empty and the leader had the headman, and Huo Tieshan had never in his life let a thing he was responsible for die in trade for an advantage.

The spear-work, the grey men told each other afterward — the two who lived — had been the worst of it. The boar spear is a peasant weapon, a hedge weapon, and the man wielded it like the border sergeant he had been, short and ugly, crosspiece and butt and blade all three, and the third grey man died on it not understanding how. But the leader's hook-chain had taken the spearhead on its third pass, and a hook-chain is not a weapon at all so much as a patience: it wraps, and takes, and after it has wrapped and taken enough, the man at the other end of it walks in past a hunter's empty hands and does his work with a short blade, slow enough to ask questions while he does it.

His father did not answer any of the questions. He had a last act in him, while the blade worked: he got a hand into the leader's wounded shoulder, around the standing arrow, and drove it — and the leader's scream went up with the barn-smoke, and that unprofessional sound, that one crack in the grey, was what broke the square's spell and sent the villagers surging, doors slamming open, mattocks and flails and forty households' worth of fury too late into the firelight.

Up on the ridge, the world had gone red at the edges.

Yuan didn't notice it as seeing. He noticed it as arithmetic gone strange: he had stopped counting the men in the square and begun counting the pulse-beats in his own ears, which had become very loud, and very slow, and were saying a word on every beat, and the word was finish. His hands were in the dock weeds. His hands were full of dock weeds, roots and dirt, torn loose, and he didn't remember tearing them. The drum where my pulse should be, some last librarian part of him recited, from a tannery a hundred years ago — then the words go out of you —

He found the rope because he had braided it nightly for a year.

Arrows: two loosed, two dead. Third arrow: turned, lodged. Grey men: six. Standing: three. No — two standing in the square, one gone four-legged into the dark, gone where, gone after WHAT—

The count caught, and held, and hauled him back hand over hand out of the red — and delivered him, shaking, soaked, seven years old, to the exact thought the Tide had been hiding from him: the hound is casting my trail, my trail crosses the ford, the ford is where Ge is. His father was down by the well with the village around him. Between the ridge and the well stood nothing Yuan could do that the Tide hadn't wanted. Between the ridge and the ford stood his father's last order.

He went down the dark side of the ridge like water.

He smelled the tannery before the ford, and then he smelled the other thing: a sweetish carrion-and-myrrh stink moving on the night air, downwind of him — downwind, placed downwind, and a voice in his bones that was nobody's voice but his own now said: it let you smell it. It is moving you like a beater moves a boar. Toward the man behind you.

There was a man behind him. He had picked the man up two hundred paces back, a road-walker's tread trying to be quiet on forest duff, pushing him, while the hound swung wide to hold him on the path — and the path led to the dry stones, the crossing, the open ford where a boy mid-creek is the easiest meat in the world.

Act the hunt, said the year of him that had been built in a stinking yard. They think they're driving prey. Be the ground the prey runs over.

The game trail forked at the lightning oak. He had set the springpole snare on the left fork in the fourth month — entry ninety-one in the book, one of nine, checked and reset eight days ago, sized for boar, because Old Ge had said set them for boar, cub, and pray you never learn why — and Yuan went down the left fork loud. Loud for him: a snapped stick, a breath, the flicker of a small shape between trees, prey-sign laid out like coins to a miser. The road-walker took the fork at a run, blade out, eyes on the small fleeing shape and never once on the ground, because the ground in a forest at night belongs to whoever was there in daylight, and he was a man who had walked roads all his life.

The springpole took him at the ankle and turned the world over. He went up and sideways into the dark with a whip-crack of green birch, and the noise he made was not a scream, it was confusion — the world does not do this — and then he was swinging head-down with his blade lost in the leaf litter, and Yuan came out of the dark he was of.

He was a hunter's son and a tanner's apprentice. He knew where bodies came apart. It took one motion, the skinning knife exactly where the hides had taught him a throat was, and it was over before the man's grey hood had stopped swinging.

He did not stay to watch. That, too, was the craft, laid into him so deep it moved him before thought: you do not stand admiring at a kill site with a second hunter in the dark. He faded off the trail and was halfway to the ford, knife still in his fist, when the night ahead of him bloomed white.

Not fire — powder. A cloud of it, ghost-pale under the moon at the tannery fence, and out of the cloud a shriek that didn't belong in a dog or a man or anything with an honest skeleton, and the heavy iron-shod sound, twice, of a staff coming down the way a soldier brings it down, full from the shoulder, on something on the ground. Then nothing. Then the creek, saying the one thing it knew.

Yuan came to the fence low and ready and found Old Ge standing in the settling lime dust over a thing that had stopped being anything, the staff's iron shoe wet, his bad leg trembling under him in a way Yuan never mentioned to him afterward, not once, not in all the years.

The old man's eyes found him, went over him — the knife, the blood that was not his, the soaked shaking smallness of him — and something in the gullied face broke and reset itself in the space of a breath, into flat command.

"How many."

"Six came." Yuan's voice worked on the second try. "Two dead at the well — Father's arrows. One dead on the spear. One in my snare on the oak fork." He looked at the ruin in the lime dust. "That's five."

"The last?"

"The leader. Arrow in his shoulder, driven deep." The red tried to come up at the edges again; he counted it down, four heartbeats, five. "Father drove it."

Old Ge looked at him for a long moment, over the dead hound, through the dust.

"Where's your father, cub," he said, gently, knowing the answer; he had heard the square as well as Yuan had.

Huo Tieshan died a little before midnight, on a door laid flat by the well, with the village around him and the barn burning down to a red eye behind, and his son's hand held in the great scarred wreck of his own.

He had waited. The wound said he shouldn't have, the herbalist said he couldn't have, and he had waited anyway, because there was an account open and the man had never once in his life left an account open. When Yuan knelt into the torchlight his father's eyes found him and cleared.

"Count me," his father said. Barely sound at all. "The well. Two. The spear—"

"One. And one on the oak fork. Mine." Yuan held his hand in both of his now, around the missing joint of the ring finger, the way the hand had once swallowed his whole scar. "Four, Father. Of six. And the leader's marked, shoulder, deep. He's walking back grey-faced with one man and no hound and nothing else. Nothing else." His voice held because it had to. "The account's good."

"The account's good," his father breathed. Something that had been working in his face settled, hearing the numbers, the way Yuan settled nightly over the book; and Yuan understood, in that moment and forever, where the counting had really come from, who had carried it home from the border in his bones and laid it down in his son without either of them knowing. "The chest. Under our bed. Yours now — should have… long ago. Your mother's in it." A breath. "Mind Ge. Mind the third part." A longer breath. The swift rare smile, last of all, fainter than the torchlight: "Crooked arrow… flew true."

The creek said the one thing it knew. The swallows were long asleep. Yuan knelt holding his father's hand until it was only weight, and around him forty households stood with their torches and their mattocks and their shame, and he did not cry then, because there was a count running in him that had to be kept or the red would come up, and it went: one. One. One. One.

They burned the dead two days later on the shrine hill — the headman lived; the miller's brother and old Chen of the mill-race did not, and Huo Tieshan went to the fire between them, a neighbor between neighbors, which is what he would have asked. The grey men's bodies they burned downwind, separately, without a word said over them, and Old Ge ground what was left of the hound into the lime pits himself.

The iron chest was banded oak, army-made, and Yuan had never once seen it open. On top, folded in oilcloth: a discharge paper, Heaven Dou border army, Sergeant Huo Tieshan, twelve years' service, mustered out with honor — and a commendation line, in a clerk's faded hand, for the action at Ash Hollow, against the grey heresy, in which the sergeant's conduct preserved his company. Beneath the paper, wrapped in a child's winter sock long outgrown: a disc of grey iron on a cord, stamped with a brand of nine radiating lines, the ninth line broken — and a note in his father's square careful characters, dated the year of Yuan's birth:

Took this off the one I killed at the Hollow. The Hall said burn everything grey. I kept it. A soldier keeps proof of the worst thing he's seen, or stops believing he saw it, and goes back to sleeping easy, and is no use to anyone when it comes again. It will come again. — H.T.

And beneath that, in a nest of his mother's letters that Yuan did not read that night, because a person can only carry so much: her pendant. A teardrop of some stone neither red nor black but both, warm to the touch — warm the way the shard had been warm, the way the candle had been warm — on a fine chain mended twice with wire. He closed his fist around it, and the crescent scar lay against the stone, and for just a moment the itch of the scar went out entirely, like a noise he'd lived with so long he only heard it by its stopping.

He put the cord around his neck, under his shirt. He wrote the chest's contents in the book, every item, and the count of the dead, and the date. Then he sat with the pencil a long time, and at the bottom of the page, in the big careful characters the village school had given him, he opened a second ledger that no one would read for many years, with a single entry:

Owed: one father. One mother (suspected; unproven). Debtor: the grey men. The account stays open.

Old Ge found him there at moonrise, sitting in the doorway of the empty house with the book on his knees. The old man lowered himself onto the step, took out his pipe, didn't light it.

"The leader lived," Yuan said. "I should have—"

"You should have done exactly as you did, and your snare bought us the only thing worth having, which is time. But hear what comes now, cub, because it comes sure as frost." Old Ge looked down at the village, the gap in its roofline like a pulled tooth, the smell of wet char that wouldn't leave till spring. "A marked man walks back to his masters with this story: the shard-nest was cold, the village knew nothing, and the cold nothing village somewhere in it had a sergeant of Ash Hollow, an old man, and a trap-line, and it cost him four of six. Grey men are not like other men; loss doesn't anger them, it interests them." He put the cold pipe in his teeth. "They'll mark the village answered. Then they'll come back to count."

The creek said the one thing it knew. Yuan closed the book.

"Then we shouldn't be here for the counting," he said. "When do we leave?"

And Old Ge, who had been dreading the saying of that exact thing to a boy two days an orphan, looked at him sidelong for a while, and what was in the gullied old face was not approval and was not sorrow. It was recognition.

"Dawn," he said.
