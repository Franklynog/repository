Chapter 4 — The Ashwood

Lu Yan, age 6 · Spirit Apprentice (rank 13, concealed) · 0 rings · POV: Lu Yan · The Ashwood, western wildlands

The Ashwood was not named for fire. It was named for what grew there — pale ash-trees with bark the grey of old bone and leaves that hung even in winter, a dim, soft, perpetual grey that swallowed the morning light a hundred paces past the tree-line and never gave it back. The frost did not reach the forest floor. The forest floor reached up instead: a deep, dry, whispering carpet of fallen grey leaves that had not rotted because nothing here was in a hurry, that took a footstep and held it and let it go again with a sound like a long slow exhale.

It was beautiful, in the way that the inside of a mouth is beautiful before it closes.

Lu Yan walked point because the trance let him, and the others walked in the cone of his stillness whether they understood it or not. He had learned that too, this winter, feeding the furnace in the dark — that the war-trance was not only for the moment of killing. He could hold it low and banked, a candle instead of a forge, and in that low cold state the wood arranged itself for him: the wrong silences, the places where the leaf-carpet had been disturbed and re-settled, the faint sour-musk seam of a game trail, the slow change in the air's weight that meant the ground was about to fall away toward water. He read the Ashwood the way Old Cinder read iron. He had walked into it expecting to be prey. He found, two hundred paces past the tree-line, that some cold instinct older than either of his lives had already started teaching him to hunt.

"It's watching us," Mei Sang said quietly, an hour in. She walked at his left shoulder with her healer's soul half-summoned, a faint green mist clinging to her hands, useless as a weapon and worth more than any weapon, because she could feel the wood's mood the way he read its tracks. "Not one thing. The whole — all of it. Like a room where you've just stopped talking."

"I know," Lu Yan said.

"You're not afraid."

"No."

"I am," Mei Sang said, simply, without shame. "I'm going to be afraid the whole time, and I'm coming anyway, and I want you to remember that's a thing a person can do. Be afraid and choose anyway. In case you ever need to know what it looks like."

Lu Yan filed it. He was, he had begun to notice, filing a great many things she said, in a cabinet that was getting its own quiet shelf.

Behind them Ke Lang carried a frontier spear too big for him and a fear too big for the spear, and Dou and Pip walked in the middle where the middle was safest, and the grey wood breathed around all of them, and held their footsteps, and let them go.

The cheap rings announced themselves all morning, exactly as Old Cinder had promised.

A white-flecked moss-hare the size of a dog, ten or twenty years old, that froze in a fern-break and watched them with too-knowing eyes — a beast-soul host with a single soul skill, a hare that could blur three steps in a heartbeat. Easy. White ring. Pip's eyes had gone wide and hungry at it: that one, take that one, that one won't kill us. Lu Yan walked past it, and the moss-hare, reading his stillness for what it was, did not blur away. It simply lowered itself slowly into the ferns and let the dangerous quiet thing pass, the way the small wise always let the cold pass.

A bristle-sow in a wallow near the water, sixty or seventy years, yellow ring, mean and territorial and exactly the kind of kill an elder would call appropriate for a gifted six-year-old. Ke Lang had tensed at it, half-eager — that's a boar, that's my kind, I could help with that one. Lu Yan watched the sow heave up out of her wallow, read her age in the yellow cast of her tusks and the slow weight of her, and judged her not enough, and led his five children in a wide silent arc around her wallow while she huffed and stamped and decided, in the end, that whatever was passing through her wood was not worth the cost of meeting it.

That was the lesson he had brought from the forge and it held all morning, in beast after beast: the wood reads me the way I read it. The cheap things, the ten-year and fifty-year and hundred-year things, looked at the small cold creature moving through their grey home with a furnace banked behind its eyes, and the wise ones got out of the way, and the foolish ones he could have killed and did not, because Old Cinder was right and he had decided to believe him: the floor he laid at six would hold up the rest of his life, and he would not build his life on cheap stone.

He went deeper. The grey closed in. The leaf-carpet got older underfoot, and softer, and quieter, until even Mei Sang's frightened breathing seemed loud, and Ke Lang had stopped pretending he wasn't terrified and started just being terrified silently, which Lu Yan respected more.

And in the deep heart of the Ashwood, where the ash-trees grew thick as a fist and the grey light failed to nothing and the leaf-carpet was so old and so deep that a grown man would have sunk to his knees in it, Lu Yan found the thing he had come for, and it found him at the same moment, and it was not afraid of him at all.

It had been a wolf, once, the way Old Cinder had been a wall.

Now it was something the wood had been making for a very long time. Lu Yan's first sense of it was wrongness — a silence too total, a place where the wood's ambient unease simply stopped, the way sound stops at the edge of deep water. Then the smell: not musk, not rot, but a cold mineral scent, like wet stone, like the inside of a cave, like nothing that should come off a living animal. Then, last, because it had willed it last, the thing itself, rising up out of the deep grey leaf-carpet where it had lain folded and patient and perfectly still, perhaps for years.

An ashen direwolf, the wood-lore would have called it, if anyone in Slagford had lived long enough in its part of the wood to name it. It stood as high at the shoulder as a frontier pony, and it was the grey of the ash-bark and the grey of the dead leaves and the grey of the failing light, so that even moving it seemed half made of the wood itself. Its eyes were the only color in the deep heart of the Ashwood — a pale, cold, lampless blue, old and patient and entirely without fear, the eyes of a thing that had been the apex of its small grey world for longer than Slagford had stood. Around its paws, where it set them down, the leaf-carpet did not exhale. It crunched, frost-rimed, because the cold came off the beast in a slow steady tide.

Lu Yan read its age the way the forge had taught him to read heat, and the number the reading gave him was the number he had come for and a little more than he had bargained on, and the part of him that did arithmetic noted it with cold approval and a thin clean edge of something that was almost respect.

Old. Five hundred years. Six. A purple ring — past purple, near the top of it. Far beyond what any elder would call appropriate for a six-year-old. Far beyond what a sane child should attempt. The kind of ring a gifted soul master might hunt at twenty, with a team, with care. The kind of floor that would hold up a very tall house.

The kind of thing that could, very easily, kill him.

"Lu Yan." Mei Sang's voice was a thread. The green mist had gone out of her hands; her whole small body had locked, the prey-stillness, the freeze. Behind her Ke Lang had made a sound that was not a word. Dou and Pip had stopped existing as anything but two held breaths. "Lu Yan, that's — we have to go, we have to go now, that's not a ring, that's a —"

"Take them back," Lu Yan said.

He did not raise his voice. He did not look away from the lampless blue eyes. He had already begun, with the slow deliberate care of a man drawing a knife from a known drawer, to reach for the thing under his skin.

"Mei Sang. Take them. Back along the seam, the way we came, walk don't run, running wakes the cheap things. Get them to the tree-line." The cold was coming up in him now, the trance, not flooding but clarifying, the wood and the wolf and the five children and his own pulse all arranging themselves into a single clear cut-glass picture in which there was exactly one path that ended with him alive. "If I'm not at the tree-line by full dark, I'm not coming. Don't wait. Don't come back in. Take them home."

"You can't —" Mei Sang started.

"I'm afraid," Lu Yan said, which was a lie, the only lie he had told her, and he told it because it was the one word that would make her move. "You said be afraid and choose anyway. I'm afraid, Mei Sang, and I'm choosing. Now you choose. Get them out."

It was a cruel thing to do, using her own courage as a lever to make her leave. He knew it was cruel. He did it anyway, because nothing in him recoiled from it and four children's lives were worth one girl's hurt, and that arithmetic, too, he filed, in the shelf that was becoming her own, and did not look at.

Mei Sang made a sound he would remember. Then she grabbed Pip's hand and Dou's collar and hissed at Ke Lang, and the four of them went back along the seam into the grey, walking, not running, because even terrified she was smarter than her fear — and Lu Yan was alone in the deep heart of the Ashwood with a thing five hundred years his elder, and the cold came up the rest of the way, and the world went quiet, and clear, and his.

The direwolf watched the children leave and did not chase them.

It had already chosen its meal. It was looking at the small cold thing that had not run.

The maul was wrong for this. Lu Yan had known it before he came; Old Cinder had known it for him. The maul was an army's weapon, a single tremendous blow that cost him a day's strength to summon and could not be aimed at something that moved like falling shadow. He left it where it lived, in the red place inside him, and he took Old Cinder's axe in his right hand, plain frontier iron honed to a line of light, real iron that did not tire and did not cost, and he let the crucible give him only the one thing it gave for free: the war-trance, and the strength to use a child's body past what a child's body should bear.

The wolf came without a sound.

It did not gather itself or growl or give any of the warnings a lesser beast would give. One instant it stood in the frost it made; the next it was simply arriving, a tide of grey and cold crossing the deep leaf-carpet faster than the eye that was not in the trance could follow — and Lu Yan, who was in the trance, who stood in the cold clear still place where the world slowed to honey, watched it come and read the line of its leap and was not where the line ended.

He moved a half-step. The leaf-carpet exhaled where his foot left it. The wolf's jaws closed on the grey air his throat had occupied a quarter-heartbeat before, cold radiating off them in a wave that frost-burned the side of his face, and as it passed he set Old Cinder's axe into the long grey muscle of its shoulder with everything a six-year-old body lent past its breaking, and felt the true iron bite, and felt, distantly, the hot shock of his own small joints tearing with the force of the blow.

The wolf screamed. It was the first sound it had made and it was terrible — not a howl, a high cold splitting note like the crystal had made, like ice breaking on deep water — and it twisted in the air with the axe buried in it and came down wrong and spun, and its tail, heavy as a club and rimed with frost, took Lu Yan across the chest and threw him ten feet into the bole of an ash-tree.

Ribs went. He felt them go — felt the wet crack of it, the white edge of pain that in any ordinary state would have ended him as a fighter. The trance took the pain and set it aside, not gone, filed, a thing to deal with later if there was a later. He was already moving. He had to be already moving. He had read this part too, in the cold clear picture: a thing five hundred years old does not give you a second clean blow, so the first one has to start something the second one finishes, and he had aimed the first blow not to kill but to cripple the leg, and the wolf, coming around for him again, came around slow, dragging the shoulder he had opened.

That was the whole of his plan and the whole of his chance. Cripple it, and outlast it, and let it bleed in the cold it made, and find — in the long terrible minutes of staying alive against a thing that should kill him in seconds — the single instant when five hundred years of patience finally, fatally, lapsed.

He found it in the dark. He was never sure, afterward, how long it took. The trance ate time the way it ate pain. There was the wolf and the cold and the grey, and his own breath sawing wet and red, and Old Cinder's axe biting again and again into grey muscle wherever the cold clear picture showed him an opening — the other shoulder, the flank, once, gloriously, the soft place behind the foreleg where real iron found something that mattered. There was the tree-bark at his back when he ran out of room and the leaf-carpet sucking at his feet when he ran out of tree, and the slow terrible arithmetic of a child bleeding out a little slower than a wolf, each of them spending what they had, each of them counting.

And at the end — when the lampless blue eyes had begun, at last, to gutter, when the frost off the beast had thinned to almost nothing, when it gathered itself one final time for a leap it no longer had the leg to make — Lu Yan did the thing he had been saving, the thing the maul was for, the thing that cost him a day's strength and that he had been holding in the red place since the children left.

He summoned the maul into the wolf's last leap.

Not to swing it. He had no strength left to swing anything; his arms were ruined, his ribs were a wet ruin, the axe had fallen from a hand that would not close. He summoned the great black war-maul of cooling slag directly into the air along the line of the wolf's final desperate arc, a tremendous immovable weight of soul-forged iron materializing in the one place the cold clear picture had promised the wolf would be —

— and the ashen direwolf, five hundred years the apex of its grey world, came down out of its last leap with all its failing weight and broke itself against the maul like a wave against a seawall.

The sound it made was not a scream this time. It was very small, and very final, and then it was nothing at all, and the deep heart of the Ashwood was silent in a new way, a way it had not been silent in five hundred years, and in that silence a six-year-old boy lay broken in the deep grey leaves beside the cooling body of the thing he had killed, and breathed, wet and slow and alive, and felt the trance begin, gently, to recede, and the pain begin, patiently, to arrive.

He had perhaps a minute before the pain became the whole world. He had perhaps a minute before the body that had carried him here failed.

And the wolf was dead, and the wolf was warm, and the channels of the crucible were already turning at his back, opening of their own slow hunger toward the great cooling five-hundred-year life laid out beside him in the dark —

— and the ring, the spirit ring, the floor he had come to lay, had a window, and the window was closing.

Lu Yan, broken in the leaves, did the last arithmetic of the longest day of his second life, and reached.
