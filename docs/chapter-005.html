Chapter 5 — The Floor He Laid

Lu Yan, age 6 · Spirit Master (rank 14, concealed) · 1 ring (purple) · POV: Lu Yan · The Ashwood

There is a thing that happens when a soul master absorbs a spirit ring, and every account of it in either of Lu Yan's worlds described it as a kind of grace — a warmth, a homecoming, the dead beast's strength flowing into the living master like water finding its level, the new ring settling around the body in a band of light. Gentle. Generous. The reward at the end of the hunt.

That is not what the crucible did.

The wolf's spirit ring rose from its cooling body as a band of deep, royal, near-black purple — past the middle of purple, near its top, the color of a thing that had lived five hundred years and meant to live five hundred more — and it drifted toward Lu Yan the way such rings drift, slow, almost tender, ready to be received.

The crucible did not receive it.

The crucible took it, and it took the wolf with it, and it took, Lu Yan understood in the long white instant of the absorption, far more than the wolf.

The channels opened all the way. Not the thimble's-width he used on rats in the forge-dark, not the cup's-width of a slag-cat — all the way, the full hungry mouth of the thing he carried, because a five-hundred-year life was not a thimble or a cup, it was an ocean, and the crucible meant to drink the ocean. The purple ring did not settle around him gently. It was seized, hauled in, slammed into place around his broken body like a manacle closing, and the wolf's cooling strength did not flow — it poured, a torrent of cold patient ancient power forced down channels far too small for it, and the forcing tore him, and the forcing healed him, both at once, ribs knitting wrong and then right under the flood, ruined joints screaming and mending, the whole broken architecture of a six-year-old body remade in the space of a single held breath into something that could hold what was being poured into it — barely, agonizingly, at a cost.

And the cost went where it always went.

He felt it leave him. That was the part the accounts never mentioned, because no account had ever been written by a soul master who carried what Lu Yan carried. The absorption of an ordinary ring spends a little of the beast's strength simply in the joining — a tax, paid out of the dead thing's surplus, small enough that no one notices. The crucible's joining was not small. To force the wolf's ocean down channels built for cups, to seize a near-black purple ring into a body that should not have survived it, the crucible needed to open itself wider than it had ever opened — and the cost of opening, the cost it never paid itself, went out of Lu Yan in a wave he could feel, a cold front of pure displaced spending that rolled off him into the deep heart of the Ashwood in every direction at once.

He lay in the grey leaves and felt it go, and he heard the wood die.

It was not loud. That was the horror that lived in him longest, longer than the broken ribs, longer than the lampless blue eyes: it was not loud at all. It was a sound like a vast slow exhale, like the leaf-carpet's small sigh under a footstep but multiplied past counting, a soft grey susurration spreading outward through the deep wood as the cost of his ring came due on everything that lived and could not stop him. The ash-trees did not fall. They simply stopped — the grey leaves that had hung through winter loosing all at once, a slow grey snow of them sifting down through the dark; the deep old leaf-carpet going from soft to brittle; the moss on the boles going white; the small wise things that had gotten out of his way all morning, the moss-hares and the slag-cats and the patient cold beetles in the deep loam, going still in their burrows in a widening circle with the dead wolf and the living boy at its center.

Not all of it. Not the whole Ashwood — the crucible was not a god yet, and an ocean could only buy so wide a grave. But a circle. A great circle of the deep heart of the wood, three hundred paces across, perhaps four, gone grey and brittle and still in the space of a single absorption, so that when the torrent finally ebbed and the purple ring sat hard and cold and his around a body remade to hold it, Lu Yan pushed himself up out of the dead leaves and stood, healed, ringed, a true soul master at six years old — at the dead center of a circle of forest he had murdered to pay for it.

He stood there a long time.

He did the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was the only thing he had ever been able to feel with any urgency, and the arithmetic, this time, was very large, and very clear, and it said: one ring. Near-black purple. The floor of a very tall house. Old Cinder was right; this floor will hold up anything. Cost: this. All of this. And he made himself look at the all-of-this, the grey circle, the snow of dead leaves still sifting down through the dark, the small still shapes in the loam — made himself look, because he had decided in the tallhouse loft that the one discipline he would not allow himself to lose was the discipline of seeing the bill. A man who looks away from the bill, he had reasoned, is a man who will eventually convince himself there is no bill, and a man who believes there is no bill will spend without limit, and a man who spends without limit, with a furnace like this, becomes the thing the story warned about — not through madness, since the madness had been spared him, but through ease. Through never once being made to feel the price.

So he looked. He stood at the center of the circle of his first great spending and he looked at every part of it, and he filed it, all of it, in the cold cabinet, under the heading he had written at six and would carry to the end: Cost. Displaced. Real. This is what one ring weighs. Remember the weight. Whatever else you let yourself forget, furnace child — remember the weight.

Then, because the light was failing and Mei Sang would not leave the tree-line no matter what he had told her, because he knew her now well enough to know that, he turned his back on the grey circle and went to find his people.

The new soul skill came up his arm as he walked — unbidden, the way the maul had come unbidden in the cold yard, the crucible showing him what it had bought. From the deep near-black purple ring, a band of cold blue rimed his right forearm, and the leaf-litter ahead of him whitened with sudden frost where his focus fell, and he understood the shape of the gift without being told: the wolf's patience, the wolf's cold, the wolf's five-hundred-year stillness, made into a thing he could spend. Hoarfrost Tread, he named it, privately, for the way the frost ran out ahead of him along the ground — a soul skill of cold and patience and the slow killing stillness of the deep wood, a hunter's skill, a predator's skill, exactly suited to the thing he had paid so much to become.

It was a good skill. It was a magnificent floor. He would build a magnificent house on it.

He did not let himself enjoy it. He had decided that too, walking out of the grey circle: that he would never let himself enjoy the spending, because enjoyment was how ease became appetite. He would use the power. He would climb the ladder. He would let the world pay, because he had been handed a soul that gave him no other way to climb — but he would not, ever, let himself like it. That thin cold discipline, self-imposed, unenforced by anything in his own flat nature, was the only leash Lu Yan had, and he gripped it the way Old Cinder gripped the memory of a hammer he could no longer lift, and for the same reason: so that he would always know it was real.

Mei Sang was at the tree-line in the last grey light, exactly where he had told her not to be, with the other three huddled behind her and her healer's soul lit full and trembling, the green mist streaming off her hands into the dark as if she could push it all the way to the deep heart of the wood by wanting it badly enough.

She saw him coming out of the grey — small, whole, walking, a near-black purple ring turning slow around him in the dark, frost running out ahead of his feet — and her face did something he had never seen it do. Relief and terror at once, the two of them fighting, neither winning.

"You're alive," she said. And then, because she was Mei Sang, because she felt things the way he could not and had appointed herself the keeper of the shape of them: "Lu Yan, the wood — I felt it — back there, behind you, I felt all of it just — stop. All at once. Like a candle. A whole — a whole part of the wood, just gone out. What did — what was that, what did you —"

"That was the cost," Lu Yan said.

He did not lie to her. He had lied to her once, in the deep wood, to make her save the others, and he had decided that was the only lie he would spend on her, because she was the one person in two lives who looked at the flat grey wrongness of him and stayed, and a man does not lie to the only thing that stays. He stopped in front of her in the failing light, small and whole and ringed and terrible, and he told her the truth, in his flat quiet voice, because she of all people had earned it and because someone, he had decided, should know — should carry the shape of it the way she carried the shape of grief, so that if he ever forgot the weight there would be one person left in the world to remember it for him.

"My soul takes life and turns it to strength," he said. "And it makes the world pay the cost of the taking, because it will not make me pay it. I killed the wolf. That was mine to do; I came to do it. But the ring cost more than the wolf, and the more, the wood paid. A circle of it. Three hundred paces, maybe four. It's dead, Mei Sang. I killed it the way I'd snuff a candle, by being what I am, and it cost me nothing — nothing — and that is the whole truth of me and I'm telling it to you because you're the only one who'll feel it the right way, and I need one person in the world to feel it the right way, because I can't."

Mei Sang stared at him in the dark. The green mist guttered around her hands. Behind her Ke Lang and Dou and Pip had gone very still, hearing a thing children should not have to hear.

And Mei Sang — six years old, frightened, a healer to the marrow, looking at a boy who had just told her he could murder forests without feeling it — did the thing that would define the whole rest of both their lives, the thing that no arithmetic in Lu Yan's cold cabinet had predicted and none would ever fully explain.

She did not run. She did not recoil. She looked at him with her green-flecked eyes streaming and her whole small body folded around something far heavier than she was, and she said, in a voice that shook and did not break:

"Then I'm not going anywhere."

"Mei Sang —"

"No. Listen." She scrubbed the back of her hand across her face, furious, frightened, absolutely certain. "You just told me the worst thing about yourself like you were reading it off a list. You looked at a dead wood and you came back and the first thing you did was tell me the bill. You didn't hide it. You didn't lie." Her voice cracked, held. "A person who couldn't feel anything wouldn't care if I knew. A person who was really what you keep telling me you are wouldn't need somebody to feel it the right way. But you do. You said it yourself. You need one person to carry the weight, because you can't." She took a breath that was half a sob and squared her small shoulders and made it. "So I'll carry it. That's a healer's job. You break the world and I'll carry what it weighs, and I'll keep telling you the shape of it for as long as you keep telling me the truth, and the day you stop telling me the truth is the day I leave, and not one day before. That's the trade." She held out her hand, scraped and shaking, palm up, the way Ke Lang had spat in his and held it out at the granary wall, the frontier's oldest contract. "Buy me too. Same as the others. But my price isn't fists or a back. My price is you never lie to me about the bill. Ever. That's what I'm worth."

Lu Yan looked at the offered hand for a long moment in the dark, the way he had looked at Old Cinder's axe, the way he had looked at the dead grass.

He had bought Ke Lang with shelter and Pip and Dou with fear, and those purchases had cost the world nothing, he'd noticed — the moss had not died on the granary wall. Earned things grew the moss instead of killing it; he had filed that and not understood it. He understood it now, standing at the tree-line of a wood he had murdered, looking at a girl who was offering to carry his weight for the price of his honesty. He understood that there was, perhaps, a kind of taking that did not displace its cost — a kind that made instead of drank — and that the crucible had two faces, the mouth and the forge, and that he had spent six years feeding the mouth and had not once, until this moment, understood what the forge was for.

He took Mei Sang's hand.

The moss at the tree-line, the first green moss past the dead grey circle, did not die.

He noticed. He filed it. And for once — just once, in the failing grey light, with a purple ring turning around him and a dead wood at his back and a frightened girl's scraped hand in his — he let himself not look away from the thing it meant.

Then the light failed the rest of the way, and the cheap things began to wake in the living parts of the wood, and there were four other children and a long walk home in the dark, and Lu Yan the soul master, age six, one ring, the floor of a very tall house laid in near-black purple, gathered his people and led them east out of the Ashwood toward Slagford, and the spring, and the Tallyman, and everything that was coming.

Behind them, the grey circle stood silent in the dark, and did not grow back.

It never would. Lu Yan would come this way again, years later, a Spirit King and then a Spirit Emperor and then something with no name a frontier town could hold, and the circle would still be there, grey and brittle and exactly four hundred paces across, a dead place in a living wood, and he would stand in the center of it and remember the weight, because he had promised himself he would, and because Mei Sang, by then, would no longer be able to remember it for him.

But that was years away, and the dark was now, and the boy walked home to lay the second course of stone on the floor he had bought, and did not look back, and felt — faintly, in the place where the rat's warmth had lived, in the place a scraped hand had just briefly filled — the smallest possible flicker of something his furnace had no channel for, and could not drink, and could not displace, and did not, for once, try to.
