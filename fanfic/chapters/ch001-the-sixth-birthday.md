# Chapter 1 — The Sixth Birthday

*Volume 1: Hunter · Huo Yuan, age 6 · Lieshan Village, Year 6*

---

Eleven arrows. Three with good fletching, six passable, two that would tumble before
twenty paces. Huo Yuan laid them out on the doorstep in the grey light before dawn and
counted them twice, because counting them once had never felt like enough.

"You'll wear the feathers out with looking," his father said from inside the house.

"The grey one's crooked."

"Then it'll fly crooked, and you'll learn to aim crooked." Huo Tieshan came out into the
doorway, ducking under the lintel the way big men learned to duck without thinking. He
had his good boots on, the ones he only wore for festival days and funerals, and he'd
oiled them until they shone like wet stones. "Put them away. No hunting today."

"It's only a ceremony."

"It's only your sixth birthday, and it's only the day the whole village finds out what
you are." His father took the arrows from him one at a time, in no hurry, and set them
in the quiver by the door. His hands were the largest things Yuan knew, scarred across
the knuckles, missing the top joint of the left ring finger. They were never rough with
him. "Wash your neck. Your mother would skin me."

His mother had been dead for two years, but in their house she was still the rule
everything was measured against. Yuan washed his neck.

---

The shrine hill stood at the east end of Lieshan, forty households' worth of village
gathered at its foot like sheep at a gate. The shrine itself was old past anyone's
remembering — a squat stone room with a swaybacked roof, built around a slab of darker
rock that the grandmothers said had fallen from the sky and the grandfathers said was
just a rock. Once a year, in spring, the Spirit Hall examiner came up the forest road on
a mule, and every child who had turned six that year climbed the hill, and the village
learned which of its children were people of consequence.

This year there were five of them. Yuan stood last in line, because the line went by
birth date and he had been born in the last month of winter, and he passed the waiting
the only way he knew how. Fifty-one villagers on the hill, not counting children under
six. Four hens that had followed the crowd up. One examiner.

The examiner was a young man in white and yellow robes that had been splendid for
somebody else first — the hem was taken up with thread that didn't match. He had set a
folding table in front of the shrine, and on the table sat the crystal: a milky sphere
on a brass stand, the size of a goose egg. Yuan had seen it once a year his whole life
and never up close.

"By grace of Spirit Hall," the examiner recited, in the voice of a man who had said it
in nine villages already that week, "all are born with a soul, and at six years the soul
wakes. Approach when your name is called. Place your hand upon the crystal. Do not be
afraid. The crystal does not judge." He paused at the end the way people pause when
they've made a joke for themselves. Of course it judged. Judging was the whole of it.

Wei Dahu went first, the miller's son, big for six. His martial soul came up out of him
in a shimmer of yellow light and turned out to be a millstone. The crowd made a kind
sound. The examiner made a note. "Tool soul. Millstone. Soul power, level one." A farmer
soul, then, like his father — a life of grinding, no rings, no rank, no road out of
Lieshan. Wei Dahu's mother hugged him anyway, hard enough to lift his feet off the
ground, and Yuan watched that and filed it away without a word for what it was he was
feeling.

Second, the herbalist's daughter, a sprig of bitterleaf — useful, the examiner allowed,
in a small way. Third, a hoe. The crowd's kindness was wearing thin by the hoe; somebody
near the back laughed and was elbowed quiet.

Fourth, Liu Yan, the headman's granddaughter, put her hand on the crystal and the air
went cold and bright, and a hawk made of pale light spread its wings above her, every
feather distinct as cut paper.

"Beast soul," the examiner said, sitting up. "Wind hawk. Soul power—" The crystal had
gone a clear, ringing blue. "Level four. Innate full soul power." He was writing
quickly now. "The girl is eligible for enrollment at an academy of the Hall's
designation. Spirit Hall will bear the cost."

The hill went up like a festival. Liu Yan's grandfather wept into his beard. Somewhere
in the noise the hens panicked and were chased. It took the examiner a while to restore
the line, and by the time he did, the sun was properly up and warm and there was only
one name left.

"Huo Yuan."

Walking forward took eight steps. He counted them. The crystal up close had a flaw in
its heart, a little frozen feather of white, and the brass stand was green at the seams.
The examiner smelled of mule and chalk dust. None of this is important, his father
would say, when you sight a deer you see the deer, not the leaves — but Yuan's eyes
had always grabbed everything and let go of nothing, and they did it now while his hand
went out and settled on the crystal, which was cold.

And stayed cold.

The examiner waited. The hill waited. Inside Yuan something shifted, or didn't — he
would spend years afterward trying to remember whether he had felt anything at all in
that moment, and the honest answer was: a draft. A door open somewhere, far inside,
onto a dark he had no name for. No light. No millstone, no hawk, no hoe.

"Take your hand off. Wipe it. Try again," the examiner said. Yuan did. The crystal sat
there, milky and dumb, the little feather-flaw unchanged.

"Sometimes the soul wakes late in the day," the examiner said, but he was already
writing, and the writing was short. "Stand aside. We'll test once more at the end."

There was no one after him to be the end. The hill understood that at the same moment
Yuan did, and the silence got a texture to it, like wet wool. He went and stood at the
side, next to the table with his ears roaring, and looked at his father, who stood with
his arms folded and his face as calm as a man watching weather, and who looked back at
him steadily and did not look away, which was a thing Yuan kept.

They tested him once more at the end. The examiner pressed Yuan's whole hand flat with
his own, as if more skin might help.

"No manifestation," the examiner said at last. "And no soul power. Level zero." He
hesitated, then said, not unkindly, which somehow made it worse: "It happens. One in
ten thousand, the soul is — absent. The boy is healthy otherwise. He can farm."

*He can farm.* On the way down the hill Yuan heard it travel through the crowd ahead of
him, changing shape as it went. *No soul at all. The hunter's boy. The one whose mother
died. Empty, the crystal said. Tch — and his father so strong, a pity, a pity.* Wei
Dahu's mother gave him a pancake folded in a leaf, the way you'd give one to a beggar
at the door, out of a goodness that had pity baked all through it, and he thanked her
politely because his mother was a rule and the rule held, and he carried the pancake at
his side all the way home without eating any of it.

His father said nothing until they were inside with the door shut. Then he crouched,
which brought their eyes level, and took Yuan's shoulders in those two huge hands.

"Did the glass hurt you?"

"No."

"Did it feed you, ever? Sharpen a knife? Track a sow with young so you'd know to let
her by?" His father's eyes were dark amber, like his own; in the dim of the house they
were nearly black. "It's a tool other men use to weigh other men. We're not in that
trade. A hunter's worth isn't read from glass — it's read from the snow, in the
morning, by what's still moving and what fed whom. You hear me?"

"Yes," Yuan said, and meant it, and didn't believe it, both at once, the way you can
at six.

His father studied him a moment longer. "Tomorrow we'll cut you a real bow," he said.
"Birch. You're long enough in the arm now." Which was how Yuan knew his father had
been saving the birch stave behind the rafters for the day the crystal sang for him,
and had decided in the space of one afternoon that the bow would not wait on any glass.
The roaring in his ears eased. They ate the pancake between them, halved exactly.

---

He woke in the dark because the window was red.

Not fire-red, not the leaping light of a burning byre that every forest-edge child is
weaned on fear of. This was steady. A clean, deep red lay along the windowsill like
spilled dye, and when Yuan slid out from under the quilt and looked out, he found the
color came from above — a star over the shrine hill, low and large and wrong. He knew
the night sky the way he knew his own arrows; his father had taught him the hunter's
stars before he could read. There was no star there. There had never been a star there.

It did not occur to him to wake his father, and that — he understood much later — was
not courage and not foolishness but the door again, the draft from far inside, drawing.
He put on his boots. He went up the hill in his nightshirt with the dew soaking his
ankles, and the village slept, every window dark, as if the red light fell on him alone.
Fifty-one people on this hill in the morning, he thought, and one tonight.

Under the star, the shrine was changed. The old slab — the grandmothers' sky-stone —
had split. Not shattered: opened, along a seam no one had ever found because until
tonight it had not existed, and in the seam something glowed the same red as the star,
breathing slow, like a coal at the bottom of a banked fire.

Yuan stood in the doorway for the space of forty heartbeats. He counted them. Some part
of him was already keeping the ledger that would one day have armies in it, and the
entry it made that night was honest: *I could have gone home. Nothing made me. I
wanted to see.*

He reached into the seam.

What lay in the stone was a sliver of metal the length of his finger, red-black,
neither hot nor cold, heavier than anything that small had a right to be — and the
moment his skin touched it, it *moved*. It went through his palm the way a fish goes
through a gap in a weir, smooth and total and gone, and the pain arrived afterward, a
crescent of white fire across his hand, and the world went away.

---

A battlefield, seen from above. Banners burning. A red sky and an army kneeling in
rows that ran to the horizon, and at the head of it a figure in robes the color of a
wound, holding up one hand against a darkness that was eating the stars — and then the
figure breaking, the way the slab had broken, along seams of light, into nine falling
embers. One ember fell forever, and Yuan fell with it, and a voice spoke. Not in his
ears. In his bones, the way you hear your own pulse when you've run too far.

It did not speak in words, quite, but the meaning pressed itself into him and took
shapes from what a six-year-old had: the examiner's grading voice, his father's trade,
the village's hunger for rank.

**An heir is registered.**

**Class: Hunter. The ninth Sequence of the Crimson Path.**

**Order: physical. The flesh is the temple and the weapon; no glass shall read it,
no hall shall hold it.**

**Grade: S — the highest. Sufficient, alone among the living, to climb.**

**Hunt. Act the role until the role is true. The path ascends through nine names,
and at the summit sits a dead god's empty chair.**

**The price of climbing wrongly is the Tide. The price of stopping is being eaten.
The price of the chair is everything.**

**Begin, Hunter.**

The red star went out.

---

He came to himself on the shrine floor with dawn coming grey through the doorway and
dew gone to chill in his nightshirt, and his first thought was made of his father's
training and nothing else: *how long, and what saw me while I lay here.* The slab was
whole. He put his hands on the seam and there was no seam; the stone was one stone, as
it had been for nine hundred years, and would have looked like a dream entire — except.

Except that the morning came in sharp as a knife-edge. He could hear, distinctly, three
separate birds in the oaks down the hill and the creek beyond them and a vole moving
under the shrine floor. He could smell the cold ash of the incense burner and under it
the mule, yesterday's mule, a scent gone twelve hours that hung in the room plain as
smoke. His body felt like the new bow his father had promised: cut down to the true
grain, strung, *quiet*. When he stood, he stood in one motion, without putting a hand
down, and the strangeness of that frightened him more than the voice had.

And except for his hand.

Across his right palm, where the sliver had gone in, lay a scar like a crescent moon
no bigger than a thumbnail, white-edged, pink-new, and warm. As he stared at it, it
itched once, briefly, in the direction of the forest — *in a direction*, the way no
itch he'd ever had came with a compass in it — and then lay still.

He told no one. He walked home through a village just waking, fetched the water, fed
the fire, and sat down across from his father, who looked at him a beat too long over
the rim of his tea bowl but asked nothing, because Huo Tieshan was a man who let game
come to the blind in its own time. They spent the morning on the hill pasture cutting
the birch stave, and Yuan drew it before noon — a thing that should have taken a boy
of six both arms and a knee and still failed — drew it smooth to his ear, loosed, and
put the arrow through the heart of the straw boar at thirty paces.

His father stood very still.

"Again," he said.

Yuan put the second arrow beside the first, close enough that the shafts touched. The
crooked grey one. It flew true; somehow he'd known what its crookedness would do, the
way he knew where his own fingers were.

His father came over, knelt, and took Yuan's right hand, turning it palm up — not the
bow hand; he had seen something all the same, because he had spent thirty years reading
the morning snow. He looked for a long time at the small white crescent.

"That wasn't there at the festival," he said.

"No."

"Does it hurt?"

"No." And then, because his father had not looked away from him on the hill in front
of fifty-one people, Yuan gave him what he had: "There was a red star last night. I
went up. Something — went in. There was a voice. It said—" The words came back to him
strange in his own mouth, a grading the examiner's crystal would never make. "It said
I was a hunter. Rank S. It said *begin*."

He watched his father do something he had never seen him do: turn and look at the
forest line, all of it, slow, the way he checked the treeline when a sow had gone in
wounded, as if the trees had been listening.

"We'll say nothing," Huo Tieshan said at last. "Not to the headman, not at the well.
Whatever it is — the Hall graded you a zero yesterday, and a zero is a door no one
guards." He squeezed Yuan's hand once, around the scar, swallowing it whole in his
grip. "Hm. But there's one man I want to see that hand. He's safe, and he's old, and
he knows things a village tanner has no business knowing."

They went down to the tannery by the creek that evening, into the eye-watering reek of
oak-bark liquor, where Old Ge sat mending a hide with an awl, his ruined leg propped
on a stool. The old man took Yuan's hand the way he took a hide, flat and businesslike,
and tilted it to the lamp.

The awl stopped. Everything about him stopped — and Yuan, who was already, without
knowing it, becoming the thing the voice had named him, watched the blood leave the
old man's face the way warmth leaves a body: from the outside in.

"Ge?" his father said.

Old Ge looked up. He looked at Huo Tieshan, and then for a long time at Yuan, and his
eyes were not a tanner's eyes at all.

"Close the door," he said.
