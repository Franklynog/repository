# Chapter 2 — The Tanner's Price

*Volume 1: Hunter · Huo Yuan, age 6 · Lieshan Village, Year 6*

---

The door of the tannery was oak, two fingers thick, and when Huo Tieshan shut it the
reek of the bark liquor closed around them like water over a head. Old Ge did not get
up. He sat with Yuan's hand in his lamplight and his thumb just shy of the little white
crescent, not touching it, the way a man keeps his thumb shy of a blade he respects.

"Who else has seen this?"

"No one," Yuan's father said.

"The examiner?"

"Tested him yesterday. The whole hill watched the red fellow stand up, and the glass
read zero underneath it. Both readings. The scar came last night."

"Last night," Old Ge repeated. He let the hand go and sat back, and the stool creaked
under him, and for a while the only sound was the slow glug of the soaking vats. He
was an old man in a leather apron with bark stains to the elbow, and Yuan had known him
his whole life as a fixture of the village, like the well: the tanner who paid fair for
hides, drank tea black enough to stand a spoon in, and never went to the shrine
festivals. His left leg below the knee was a thing of leather straps and bad angles. It
was said a bear had done it, before he came to Lieshan. It occurred to Yuan, watching
him now, that it was said because Old Ge had said it, and that he had been here sixty
years and no one had ever asked twice.

"Tieshan. There's rabbit snares of mine along the creek want checking," Old Ge said.

Yuan's father didn't move. "Anything you can say to my son, you can say in front of
me."

"It's not for your ears' sake, it's for your neck's. What he carries, knowing of it has
a price, and you've got a household to—" Old Ge stopped. He looked at Huo Tieshan's
face for a moment, and grunted. "And you've already decided. Soldiers. Sit down, then.
But what's said under this roof goes in a hole, and we fill the hole in."

He turned to Yuan. "Now. You went somewhere last night, cub. Tell it. All of it, in
order, and don't smooth it. The smoothing is where lies live."

Yuan told it. The red light on the sill. The hill. The seam that had never been in the
slab. Forty heartbeats counted in the doorway. The sliver, the going-in of it, the
crescent of white fire. The battlefield, the kneeling army, the figure breaking into
nine embers. The voice. When he came to the words the voice had pressed into him, Old
Ge held up a hand, took the lamp off its hook, and set it on the floor between them, so
the light came up under all their faces.

"The words. Exact."

"It said an heir is registered, and the Vestige stood for me at my waking. It said
what the glass-priests saw is the shadow of a dead god's seat, cast through my flesh —
not my power. The shape my power will fill, name by name, if I climb. It said: class,
Hunter. The ninth Sequence of the Crimson Path. My order — physical. The flesh is the
temple and the weapon, and the figure is the temple's shadow; no glass will weigh it,
no hall will hold it. It said my grade was S. The highest. That I was the only one
living who was — sufficient. To climb." The words felt too big for the tannery, for
his mouth, for six years old; he said them anyway, in order, unsmoothed. "It said to
hunt. To act the role until the role is true. It said the path climbs through nine
names, and at the summit the Vestige is no shadow, and the chair beneath it is empty.
It said the price of climbing wrong is the Tide, and the price of stopping is being
eaten, and the price of the chair is everything. Then it said: begin."

The lamp flame stood up straight in the still air. Old Ge's face, lit from below, was
a country of gullies, and nothing moved in it.

"Begin," he said at last, softly, to no one in the room. "Sixty years, and it says
*begin*." He scrubbed both hands down his face, and when they came away Yuan was
surprised to find the old man looking at him with something that was not fear and was
not pity and had grief in it all the same. "All right. Cub. Hold out the hand."

From under his bench Old Ge took a tallow candle, lit it at the lamp, and without
warning or ceremony passed the flame under Yuan's open palm — and held it there.

Yuan's father came up off his stool. "Ge—"

"Look first. Then break my arm."

The flame stood against Yuan's skin, and it was warm. That was all. Warm like a stone
that had sat in the sun, like bathwater poured too hot and already cooling. He watched
the fire wrap the edges of his own hand and felt his heart slamming somewhere far away,
and the longer it burned the calmer the heart got, as if some deep-down part of him had
checked the fire against a list and found it expected. Half of one minute. He counted.
When Old Ge took the candle away there was soot on Yuan's palm and under the soot the
skin was whole, unblistered, not even pink — and the little crescent scar shone through
the black like a moon through cloud.

His father sat back down slowly, the way a tree decides not to fall.

"That's the first proof," Old Ge said. "The priest on the hill was the second, for
any eyes that knew the red. And the glass makes a third, in its blind way: crystal
weighs what a martial soul holds, and that figure holds nothing — it isn't a soul
standing beside him, it's a shadow thrown *through* him, by what's gone into the
meat. The seat, remembering its own shape. His power doesn't pool where glass can
dip; it's in the marrow, and it will be in the marrow at rank ninety as surely as
now. And the fire makes a fourth; the Path was born in fire, and fire knows its
own." He pinched the candle out with two bark-stained
fingers. "I'll tell you what I'd have given my good leg to be told at the start, and
then I'll tell you the price of my teaching, and the price is not small.

"There is a path with nine names on it, and a tenth at the top that isn't a name, it's
a chair. Once, the whole world will have it, a god sat in it. He's dead. Dying, his
seat broke into nine shards, and the shards keep the names, and the names can be
climbed — by one. One climber in an age, cub. Not two. The thing in your hand is the
ninth shard, the bottom stair, and it has been under that shrine since before this
village had a name, and men in grey have been looking for it for nine hundred years."

"Looking," Yuan's father said. "Do they find?"

"They find." Old Ge's voice didn't change, which was itself an answer. "We'll come to
them. Cub — what the voice told you is the whole law, and you recited it like a feast-
day poem, so now we'll find out if you heard it. *Act the role until the role is true.*
What do you think it means?"

Yuan thought. In the vats, the bark liquor glugged. "When Father stalks a deer," he
said slowly, "he says: be the wind, be the grass. He doesn't mean pretend. He means —
do everything the wind would do, until the deer can't tell the difference. And then
there isn't a difference."

Something shifted in the gullied old face, the first warm thing in it all evening.
"Tieshan. Your boy may live."

"And the other half of the law," the old man went on, leaning in, and the warmth went
out like the candle. "*Act the hunt. Never become the kill.* You wear the Hunter's
name now, and the name will fit you better every year, and that is the path working —
but a name worn too hard wears back. Push it, force it, glut it, and there's a thing
that rises in the blood. The voice called it the Tide. It starts as red at the edge of
your seeing. Then a drum where your pulse should be. Then the words go out of you —
and what's left isn't a hunter, it's an appetite shaped like one." He held Yuan's eyes.
"Every climber the Tide ever took, it took the same way: not in the fighting. After.
When the fight was over and something helpless was in front of them and the drum said
*finish the count*. Cross that line once — once, cub — and there is no road back to
yourself. Now ask me how I know."

The tannery was very quiet.

"How do you know?" Yuan asked, because the question had been put in his hands, and he
was already learning that the old man did nothing, said nothing, gave nothing without
purpose.

"Because I'm the man who stopped climbing," Old Ge said. "Sixty years ago I wore the
ninth name, and then the eighth, and then the seventh, and the price of the seventh
was paid by someone else, and I took a knife to my own meridians so I'd never be
tempted to the sixth. That's all you get tonight. That's all you get for years, maybe.
But you'll know this much: when I tell you the stove is hot, I'm not reading from a
book. I'm showing you my hands."

He stood, hauling himself up on the iron-shod staff that leaned always at his bench,
and for just a moment — a heartbeat, less — he stood the way Yuan's father stood, the
way soldiers stood, weight gathered, staff held not like a crutch but like a thing
with reach. Then he was an old tanner again, shuffling to the shelf, and Yuan filed
the moment away and said nothing.

What he brought back from the shelf was a notebook. Calf-bound, palm-sized, the
leather seconds from his own vats, scarred where the hide had scarred. He put it in
Yuan's hands with a stub of charcoal pencil.

"The price of my teaching. Three parts. First: you count."

"Count what?"

"Everything. Arrows loosed, snares set, breaths held, coppers owed, kills made. Every
night, in that book, in your own hand. You think I'm giving you a chore." The old man
bent down, and his eyes at close range were shot through with rust, like iron left in
rain. "I'm giving you a rope. The Tide eats words first, cub, and numbers are the last
words to go. The night you can't remember why the count matters, the count itself will
still be there, in your own hand, telling you a man wrote this, and you can climb a
rope of numbers back to him. My partner had no rope. Write."

Yuan opened the first page, and after a moment wrote, in the big careful characters
the village school had given him: *Arrows: eleven. Three good. Snares: —* he looked
up. "How many snares do you have on the creek?"

"There it is," Old Ge said to Yuan's father, with grim satisfaction, as if a price had
been met. "Nine. Second part: from tomorrow the boy's my apprentice. Tanner's boy,
before the village and the Hall and heaven — he hauls bark, he scrapes hides, he
stinks, and everyone who looks at him sees a zero put to honest work and feels good
about the world. What he'll be doing is learning. The hides will teach his hands where
a body comes apart. The vats will teach him patience. The stink will teach him to mind
the wind, because downwind of this place even his sainted mother couldn't have smelled
a campfire. And in the forest, early and often, his father will teach him the rest,
same as he meant to. Nothing we do will look like anything. That's the craft, cub:
*nothing you do should look like anything.*"

"And the third part," Huo Tieshan said.

Old Ge was quiet a moment. "Third part's mine to pay, not his," he said. "Sixty years
I've sat on what's under that hill, telling myself custodian, telling myself the age
would pass and no climber would come and I'd die a tanner with one secret. Now it's in
a child's hand, registered, *begun* — and the men in grey have hounds that can smell a
begun thing the way I smell a green hide. They'll come. Not this year, might be. But
they'll come up that forest road one evening, and the third part of the price is that
when it happens, you two do exactly as I say without asking why." He looked from
father to son. "That one's not negotiable, and you won't like it when it falls due."

Yuan's father held the old man's gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the
way he sealed a hide-price, and that was that, because among the men Yuan came from a
nod was a contract and a contract was a law.

"Good," Old Ge said. "Now both of you out, I've a hide to finish. Cub." Yuan stopped
in the doorway. The old man had already turned back to his bench, awl in hand, a
tanner to his bones. "First lesson's at dawn. Bring the book."

---

The examiner left Lieshan two days later, and on his way out of the village he stopped
his mule at the tannery, which no examiner had ever done.

Yuan was in the yard with a scraping-beam and a green hide, six days into a stink he
was learning to live inside, when the white-and-yellow robes appeared at the fence. He
kept scraping. *Nothing you do should look like anything.*

"The Huo boy, isn't it." The examiner's voice was friendly, in the way of a man being
friendly on purpose. "Apprenticed already. That's quick."

"Mouths don't feed themselves, honored sir," Old Ge said, coming out of the shed with
his staff thumping, all stoop and squint, an old man adding up a stranger's robes into
a sum that meant trouble. Yuan had seen him walk in the yard that morning. He had not
stooped then. "The boy's strong and his hands mind him. I work him fair."

"I'm sure." The examiner looked at Yuan a beat longer. "No soreness since the testing,
boy? No fevers, lights, dreams? Has the red fellow stood again?" The questions were
laid down gently, like snares are. "Spirit Hall asks after its oddities, you know. The
Hall records everything — His Grace the Deacon in Yanshui is particular about records.
Even souls with nothing in them. *Especially* souls no one can name. A nameless thing,
you see, is a shape too."

The crescent scar lay quiet against the scraping-beam. Yuan looked up with the dull,
willing face he'd been practicing on the hides, and said: "I had a dream the millstone
chased me, honored sir."

The examiner laughed despite himself. The mule shifted. "Well. Heaven keeps the
humble," he said, already gathering his reins, a man with nine more villages and a
ledger of his own to fill, and Yuan watched the white and yellow dwindle down the
forest road and understood, with a cold clarity that had nothing of six years old in
it, that he had just been entered into a count kept by someone else.

That night he wrote in the book: *Hides scraped: four. Snares: nine, two fed.
Questions asked about me: five. Answers given that were true: none.*

---

The summer turned. He learned the tannery: the lime pits and the bate, the slick
heavy language of hides, where a deer came apart at the joints and where it held, and
his arms stopped aching by the second month, which it should have taken a year and
which he wrote down. He learned, in the grey hours before the village woke, the
other curriculum: ghost-walking on dry leaves, reading a three-day-old trail by
fingertip, lying in a blind so long and so still that a fox once stepped over his
ankles. His father taught with few words and Old Ge with many, and between the two of
them the Hunter's name settled onto him so gradually, so daily, that there was never a
morning he woke and felt it arrive. There was only, by autumn, the quiet fact that
the forest had stopped being a place he went into and become a place he was *of* —
and that the count in the book had begun to include things like: *heartbeats from
first scent of boar to sight of boar: nineteen.*

The scar itched twice that season. Once toward the deep forest, faint, on the night
something ten-year-ranked screamed at the moon and the whole village barred its
shutters. Once — briefly, sharply — toward the road.

It was a peddler who brought the word, in the ninth month, with his ribbons and
needles spread on the well-stones and his mouth running the way peddlers' mouths run,
gossip being the coin that buys supper. Pressing-tax was up in Suotuo. A two-headed
calf in Lingmu. And on the far side of the forest, in the villages along the west
road —

"— men in grey, asking at the doors. Polite as priests, grey as ash, and only ever the
one question." The peddler dropped his voice to make it carry better. "*Have any of
your children dreamed red?* They pay copper for answers. Silver, if the answer's yes."

The well-stones buzzed. Someone laughed; someone spat against omens. Yuan, hauling
bark up the creek path, did not break stride and did not look at the tannery, where
Old Ge stood in his doorway, stooped and squinting, an old man listening to peddler's
chatter — with his staff, Yuan noted, in his left hand for the first time all year.
His fighting hand free.

That night the entry read: *Grey men: west road, four villages off, moving — direction
unknown. Question asked: one. Price of a yes: silver.*

Underneath, after a while, he added: *Distance from west road to our shrine, by the
forest paths: two days. By the main road: four.*

He was six years old, and he blew out the lamp, and lay in the dark listening to his
father's even breathing across the room, and did the thing the book was teaching him
to do: he held the fear, and counted it, until it lay down.

It was two days, by the forest paths. It would be almost a year before they came. But
that was the autumn the waiting began, and ever afterward, when Yuan tried to mark
where his childhood ended, he put it there: ribbons and needles on the well-stones,
silver for a yes, and the staff quietly changing hands.
