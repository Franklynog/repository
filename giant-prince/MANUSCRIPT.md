# BEYOND THE TIMESCAPE: THE GIANT PRINCE

### *a Beyond the Timescape × Solo Leveling × One Piece (Elbaf) crossover*

*Alternate titles: "Xu Qing: Loki of the Gates" · "Beyond the Timescape: Elbaf's Shadow"*

> A cold, calculating cultivator who clawed his way past the end of time is dragged
> by a purple light into the body of a starving boy in modern Korea — where the
> System brands him with a class no hunter has ever held: the **Loki-class, the
> Giant Prince of Elbaf**. He can grow into a mountain of mythic warrior flesh and
> crush the monsters of the Gates underfoot. But Xu Qing did not survive two worlds
> by becoming a giant. He survived by being *small*. Now the quietest man alive
> carries the loudest power in the world — and he intends to wield it the way he
> wields everything: coldly, precisely, and only when the arithmetic favors him.

**Length plan:** ~295,000 words · Prologue + 84 chapters · 7 arcs
**Rating:** Mature — graphic violence, dark survival themes, a ruthless protagonist

## The Story at a Glance

**Arc 1 — Rebirth & Giant Awakening** *(ch. 1–12)* — The soul lands in the body and
the world; the Loki-class and the first partial growth; the first Gate; the first
calculated kill; registering as a "weak E-rank" to stay invisible.

**Arc 2 — Controlling the Giant** *(ch. 13–24)* — Mastering partial vs. full
transformation and the kingly presence; a tiny deniable power base; the price shows
its first teeth as the giant tries to become his default.

**Arc 3 — The Growing Hunter** *(ch. 25–38)* — Forced up into real hunter society;
guilds, reassessment, bigger Giant Feats; fame inflicted on a man who treats it as a
wound; a first thread to the timescape.

**Arc 4 — Two Anomalies** *(ch. 39–50)* — He and **Sung Jin-Woo** recognize each
other as the two things the System cannot contain. Shadow army versus literal giant.
Scale has found him; he can no longer stay small.

**Arc 5 — Giant Feats & Great Gates** *(ch. 51–64)* — Jeju Island and international
Gates run as personal Giant Feats; he becomes a National-tier reality; the legacy and
the timescape converge; the bill lands on the warmth he guarded.

**Arc 6 — The Timescape & Transcendence** *(ch. 65–74)* — The low point; the legacy
nearly wins; he descends into Elbaf and the outside-of-time both, and finds the
*limit he must choose* to come back as himself.

**Arc 7 — The Giant Prince** *(ch. 75–84)* — A final threat that demands full
giant-prince power, won not by raw size but by the limit he chooses to grow — the
fusion of the cold survivor and the mythic giant, on his own terms.

# Prologue — The Light Past Time

There is a place where time ends.

Not the way a road ends, at a wall or a cliff or a sea, where a man may stand and
look across at more world he simply cannot reach. Time ends the way a held breath
ends — into a silence that was always underneath the sound, a silence so total that
the word *silence* is already too loud for it. The cultivators of his age had a name
for the country beyond that ending. They called it the **timescape**, and they spoke
of it the way the living speak of the dead: rarely, and never after dark.

Xu Qing stood at the edge of it.

He was old now. He had let himself grow old, which was its own kind of discipline,
because there had been centuries when growing old was the most dangerous thing a man
could do and he had done it anyway, slowly, on purpose, the way he did everything —
measuring each year against the next, spending nothing he could keep, keeping nothing
he could not defend. His hair had gone the grey of cold ash. His hands, folded inside
his sleeves, were steady. They had always been steady. That steadiness had outlived
emperors.

Behind him lay the whole long road of his life, and it was a road paved end to end in
caution. He had been born in the mud of the Sorrow Lake, a starving child in a
starving district, a nobody so small the world had not bothered to learn his name. He
had survived because he was patient where other children were brave, because he hid
where they fought, because he counted the exits in every room before he counted the
friends. He had clawed upward through a hundred ranks and a thousand graves that were
meant to be his, and he had never once mistaken his own survival for safety. *Safety
is a story the strong tell the weak,* he had decided, very young, in the dark, with
someone else's blood drying on his hands. *There is only the next risk, and whether
the arithmetic favors you.*

The arithmetic had favored him for a very long time.

It did not favor him now.

He looked into the place where time ended and he understood, with the cold clarity
that had been the truest companion of his life, that there was no exit here. He had
counted them. There were none. Every road he had ever walked had had a back door, a
shadow to step into, a smaller move that bought a larger one — and this road had only
its end. Whatever lay beyond the timescape did not have a back door, because it did
not have a *back*. It had no behind and no before. It was the thing that *before* and
*behind* were made out of.

A lesser man would have wept, or raged, or knelt. Xu Qing did the thing he had always
done when the arithmetic turned against him and there was no smaller move to make.

He made the larger one.

He stepped forward, into the ending of time, because forward was the only direction
left that he had not yet spent — and a cautious man, at the very last, is simply a man
who has saved one move for when there is nothing else; and he had saved this one his
whole life without ever admitting it, the move past the final boundary, the one with
no return in its arithmetic at all.

The crossed pupils of his eyes — the strange split that had marked him since birth,
two faint crosses in the dark of his stare, the thing the fortune-tellers of the
Sorrow Lake had feared and the monks had coveted and he himself had simply *used*, the
way he used everything — caught the last light there was and held it.

And then there was no light, because light is a thing that travels, and travel is a
thing that takes time, and there was no more time.

He should have ended there. That is what the ending of time is *for*. He had made his
peace with it the only way he made peace with anything: by accepting the figures and
not arguing with them. *This is where I stop. The ledger closes. It was a long
account, and I paid more than I was paid, and I leave nothing owed.* That was as close
to a prayer as Xu Qing had ever come, and he did not believe anyone was listening, and
he was not afraid.

That was when the light reached back.

It came from *outside* — outside the ending, outside the timescape, outside the whole
shape of everything he had ever called real — and it was the wrong color. Not the gold
of cultivation, not the white of the heavens, not the black of the abyss he had stared
into so many times that he had stopped flinching. It was *purple*. A deep, bruised,
sovereign purple, the color of a wound and the color of a crown, and it came *through*
the place where nothing could come through, the way water comes through stone given
enough patience, except that this had no patience at all. It was simply *there*,
suddenly and absolutely, as if it had always had the right.

It found him in the nothing the way a hand finds a coin in the dark.

And it took him.

There was no pain, because pain takes time. There was no fear, because fear takes a
future to be afraid *of*. There was only the cold arithmetic of his mind, working even
now, even here, even as the purple light closed around the small still flame of what
he was and *pulled* — working, and arriving, with the last clarity it would ever have
in that world, at a single conclusion that he carried with him across the impossible
gap like a knife carried in a sleeve:

*This is not my world's light. Something outside has reached in. And things that reach
do so because they want.*

*Be careful,* he told himself, in the place past time, with no breath to say it and no
time to say it in. *Whatever wants you — be careful.*

It was the last thing he thought as Xu Qing, and it was the first law of the man he
would become.

---

He fell through a length of dark that had no length.

It was not falling, exactly, because falling needs a floor to fall toward and there
was none — but his mind, which had spent an entire existence translating the
unsurvivable into terms it could survive, called it falling, and so he fell. Through
purple. Through the wrong-colored deep. Past shapes that were almost there and almost
gone, vast hanging shapes like drowned mountains, like the bones of something that had
been bigger than reason, ranked in the dark on either side of his fall as if he were
dropping down the nave of a temple built for a congregation of giants.

And in the dark, as he fell, a voice found him.

It was not the purple light. The light had no voice; the light was only *want*, pure
and patient and old. The voice was something else — something *inside* the fall, or
inside *him*, woken by the falling the way deep water is woken by a stone. It was
enormous. Not loud. *Enormous*, the way a mountain is enormous when you stand at its
foot and tip your head back and back and the top is still not there. It was a voice
with grief in it, and pride, and the particular exhaustion of a thing that has waited
so long it has forgotten what it waits for and remembers only that it is still
waiting.

It spoke two words into the place where Xu Qing was no longer Xu Qing and not yet
anyone, and the two words went through him like the first light of a country he had
never seen and somehow already mourned.

***...little prince.***

And then the falling found a floor, after all.

The floor was a body.

He went into it the way a sword goes into a sheath that was cut for it — not gently,
not kindly, but *correctly*, every part of him matching every part of the dark wet
ruined thing he was suddenly inside of, as if the body had been waiting his whole
death for exactly his shape. He felt its pain become his pain. He felt its cold become
his cold. He felt a heart that had been about to stop remember, reluctantly, how to
keep going, and take up the work again like an old laborer dragged back to a job he
hated.

And he felt — underneath the pain and the cold and the rain that was somehow falling,
real rain, the first real rain of a real new world drumming on his ruined new face —
he felt the body's own life, its short cruel starving seventeen years, rise up to meet
the long cold cunning centuries of his soul.

He braced for them to tear at each other. Two hard lives, two sets of scars, two
ledgers of grief — they should have fought. He had seen souls shatter on the rocks of
a borrowed body; he had *used* that, more than once, against enemies who tried to live
past their deaths.

They did not fight.

They *fit*.

The body's despair found his caution and recognized it. The body's hunger found his
patience and lay down beside it. The body's long memory of being small, of being
beaten, of being *nothing the world bothered to name* — it found, in the deep cold
arithmetic of the man who had survived two existences by being exactly those things,
not an enemy but a *brother*. The seam between them closed without a scar. Where Xu
Qing ended and the dying boy began, there was, after a single endless instant, no line
at all.

*A perfect merge,* some cold corner of him noted, even now, even here, lying in the
rain in the alley of a world he had not yet opened his eyes upon. *Too perfect. The
light chose this body for me, or chose me for this body. Either way — be careful. A
thing that fits this well was measured first.*

The rain came down.

Somewhere far above the alley, past the rain and the rusted sky of a city he had no
name for yet, the purple light withdrew, its want satisfied for now, leaving behind
only the thing it had wanted and the long cold work of finding out why.

And in the wet dark, in a body that had been minutes from a pauper's death, a pair of
plain dark eyes opened — and in each of them, faint as a watermark, faint as a promise,
faint as a debt not yet come due, there was the cross-shaped split of a pupil that had
looked into the ending of time and come back out the other side.

Xu Qing breathed in the air of the world of the Gates.

It tasted of rust, and rain, and someone else's blood, and — very faintly, under all
of it, so faint he would not understand it for a long time — of a country of giants,
mourning.

*Inventory,* the cold voice in him said, the way it had said it ten thousand times
across two worlds, the way it would say it until whatever finally killed him.
*Inventory, and then the exits.*

He had survived the end of time.

Now he had to survive the morning.

# Chapter One — The Hollows

The first thing he learned about the new world was that it let the weak die in the
rain, same as the old one.

He lay still and learned it slowly, because moving was a thing you did *after* you
understood your situation, never during. The rain had soaked him to the bone — the
body's bone, his bone now, the distinction already meaningless. The alley under him
was concrete, cold and gritted with something that was not quite sand, and it sloped
just enough that the water ran past his cheek in a thin cold thread toward a drain he
could hear but not see. Above him: a wall of corrugated metal gone the orange-black of
old rust, and above *that*, far up, a slot of sky the dirty violet of a city that
never fully went dark.

He did not sit up. He inventoried.

*The body.* Seventeen years old. He knew the number the way he knew his own name —
which was to say, the way he now knew *two* names, the old one folded under the new
like a knife under a coat. **Seo Cheong.** The boy had been called Seo Cheong. Xu Qing
tried the shape of it in the silence of his skull and found it fit too well, the
syllables of his old name wearing the new world's clothes. He let it be. A name was a
tool. He would answer to whatever kept him alive.

The body had been beaten. He cataloged the damage without flinching, the way he had
cataloged worse on himself across two centuries: three ribs cracked, not broken; a
split scalp still leaking warmth into the rain; the left eye swelling; bruising across
the kidneys that meant someone had stood over him and put their weight behind a boot,
more than once, with the unhurried patience of a man doing a job he'd done before. The
hands were torn at the knuckles. *He fought back.* A small fact. Xu Qing noted it the
way he noted everything about the dead boy whose skin he wore — as evidence, as
inheritance. *Not a coward, then. Only outnumbered. There is a difference, and the
difference is sometimes the whole story.*

The pain was considerable. He set it aside. Pain was information; once read, it could
be shelved. The boy had not known how to do that. Xu Qing had learned it in a colder
school than this one.

*The mind.* Here was the inheritance that mattered. He did not have the boy's memories
the way one has one's own — they came to him sidelong, like a room glimpsed through a
door swinging shut. But they came. A mother, thin and coughing, in a bed that smelled
of antiseptic and defeat. A number written on official paper, large enough that the
boy had stopped being able to feel it as money and had begun to feel it as a
*sentence*: the debt, the hospital's debt, inherited the way poverty is always
inherited, by the only one left to inherit it. Men who came for the debt. A name among
those men, spoken in the boy's memory with the particular flat dread reserved for a
thing that hurts you on a schedule.

*Bae Doek-su.* The men called him *hyeongnim* and the district called him the Tallyman
and the boy had called him nothing at all, because you do not name the weather; you
only learn which way it is coming.

Xu Qing filed it. He did not feel anger. Anger was a tax the strong levied on the weak
and called justice; he had stopped paying it a long time ago. What he felt, lying in
the rain doing arithmetic on his own murder, was something colder and more useful. *A
predator runs this district. He has a schedule. He came last night and finished the
job. Which means he believes the job is finished. Which means, for some hours, he is
not looking this way.*

*A window,* he thought. *Small. But windows are what I have always lived in.*

He breathed, and took the third inventory, the one he had been saving, the way he saved
the most dangerous move for last.

*The world.*

It poured into him from the boy's memory and from the thing he had felt fall through
with him, and the cold mind of Xu Qing received it without astonishment, because
astonishment was another tax he had stopped paying. This world had *Gates*. Tears in
the air that opened onto other places, places full of monsters — beasts the world
called *magic beasts*, things made of mana and malice that came through if the Gate
was not closed in time. The world had **hunters**: men and women who had *awakened*,
who carried a power called mana the way Xu Qing had once carried *qi*, ranked from the
lowest — *E* — to the highest the tables admitted, *S*, with whispers above even that.
The world had a System. He understood this last fact least and trusted it most,
because the boy's memory held it as plain truth: that the awakened were watched over,
measured, *leveled*, by something that wrote in light on the inside of their eyes and
made the strong stronger by a logic no one had authored and no one could refuse.

Xu Qing lay in the rain and assembled the shape of it, and the shape was this: *a
world that runs on power, and on the careful management of who is allowed to have it.*

He almost smiled. He did not, because smiling was a thing the face did when it forgot
to be careful, and his face never forgot.

*I have spent two lives in exactly such a world,* he thought. *They called the power
different names. The arithmetic is the same.*

He had everything he needed to begin. He had a body, damaged but living. He had a debt
that came with a schedule, which meant a predator with a routine, which meant a routine
that could be broken. He had a world whose rules he half-knew already, the way a man
waking in a strange house still knows what a door is for. And he had the window — the
hours before the Tallyman thought to check whether the weather had finished its work.

He moved.

It was a small, deliberate, agonizing thing: rolling to one side, getting an arm under
himself, lifting the ruined body off the cold concrete an inch at a time, the way you
lift something you intend to keep. The ribs sang. He read the song and shelved it. The
drain came into view, and past it the mouth of the alley, and past *that* the Hollows
themselves —

— and that was when the world wrote on the inside of his eyes.

It came without warning, the way the purple light had come: a pane of cold blue
radiance, hanging in the air a hand's breadth before his face, made of a script he had
never seen and understood at once, the way you understand a thing that has decided to
be understood by you.

> **[ A soul has been measured against the Lattice. ]**
> **[ Anomaly detected. ]**
> **[ Anomaly detected. ]**
> **[ ...the Lattice has no row for you. ]**

Xu Qing went very still.

He had survived two worlds by treating every gift as a question and every question as
a threat until proven otherwise. He read the words without reaching for them —
*reaching* was how the weak were caught, hands out, hungry — and he noted, coldly,
three things. *One: it measured me, which means it can. Two: it found me anomalous,
which means it has a shape it expected and I am not it. Three: it has no row for me —
and it is telling me so. A thing that confesses its blind spot is either very honest or
very confident. Be careful with both.*

The blue light flickered, as if the System itself were hesitating — as if, somewhere
behind the script, something vast leafed back and back through its tables looking for
the page that should hold a man who was two souls and had walked out the far side of
the end of time, and did not find it, and decided.

The script changed.

> **[ Manual classification engaged. ]**
> **[ Cross-referencing soul-signature against external Legacy... ]**
> **[ Match found. ]**
> **[ ...Legacy claims you. ]**
>
> **[ CLASS GRANTED — UNIQUE / LEGACY ]**
> **[ Loki — Giant Prince of Elbaf ]**

And the country of giants, mourning, opened behind the words like a door.

He felt it before he understood it. A weight settled over the small beaten body in the
alley — not a pressure pushing down but a *largeness* welling up, as if somewhere under
the seventeen starved years and the cold centuries there had always been a third
thing, vaster than both, sleeping, and the words had been its name spoken aloud, and
now it was awake and it was *huge*. He felt, for one vertiginous instant, that the
alley was too small. That the city was too small. That the body around him was a coat
cut for a child and he was about to outgrow it by a factor he had no number for.

The cold mind of Xu Qing seized that instant by the throat and held it down.

*No.* The refusal was not loud. It was the quietest thing in him, and the oldest, and
the strongest. *No. I do not move because a gift tells me to. I do not grow because a
thing inside me is hungry. I have buried larger appetites than you.*

The largeness pressed. He pressed back, with nothing but the discipline of a man who
had spent two lifetimes saying *not yet* to power, and after a long moment — a moment
in which he understood, distantly, that he was wrestling something that could have
crushed armies and that the only reason he was winning was that it did not yet know how
to want against his will — the largeness *subsided*. Not gone. Banked. Like a forge
fire raked down to coals, waiting.

And in the waiting, the voice came again. The enormous one. The one with grief in it.

***There you are,*** it said, and it was not unkind, and that was the most dangerous
thing about it. ***I felt you fall through the dark, little prince. I felt the old
light spend itself to bring you. So small.*** A vast, mournful warmth, like sun on a
battlefield long after the battle. ***So small, the vessel they gave you. But the
marrow is right. The marrow was always going to be right. You will grow.***

Xu Qing did not answer it. Answering a thing was the first step toward obeying it. He
filed the voice the way he filed everything — *threat, unknown, intimate, patient* —
and gave it the same nothing he had given the swelling blue light: his attention, and
not one thing more.

But it had told him much, in those few words, and he kept what it told him. *It is old.
It is grieving. It calls me prince — which means it wants me to be one. It says I will
grow — which means the power it offers is the power to become large. And it is glad. A
thing that old, that has waited that long, is glad to see me.* He turned the fact over
in the cold light of his caution and did not like the shape of it at all. *Nothing that
has waited that long is glad for my sake. It is glad for its own. Remember that.
Whatever this 'Elbaf' is, whatever this 'Loki' was, it died wanting something, and I am
the new hands it intends to want with.*

The blue script wrote one last line and held it, steady, patient, waiting for him to be
ready to read the truth of what he now was.

> **[ Loki — Giant Prince of Elbaf ]**
> **[ A legacy of giants. Size is the least of it. ]**
> **[ You may become as large as you can pay for. ]**
> **[ The price is not where you will look for it. ]**

He read it three times.

He had read a great many promises across two lives, written by a great many powers, and
he had learned to read them the way one reads a contract drawn up by an enemy: not for
what they offered, but for the clause they hoped you would skip. And here it was, this
world's System, this hungry Legacy, this thing that had reached past the end of time to
claim him — being, in its way, more honest than any of them had ever been.

*The price is not where you will look for it.*

*No,* he agreed, silently, in the rain, in the cold, in the ruined body of a murdered
boy, with a country of giants banked to coals beneath his bones and a sovereign-purple
want still hanging somewhere far above the rusted sky. *It never is. That is the whole
craft of it. A power with no price has only hidden the bill — and a power that tells you
the bill is hidden is a power that intends for you to spend a great deal before you find
it.*

He closed the blue window with a thought, the way you close a door on a room you are not
yet ready to enter, and he finished standing up.

The Hollows opened before him.

It was a slum, and he knew slums; he had been born in one and clawed out of one and he
would have known this for what it was with his eyes shut, by the smell alone — wet rust,
cooking oil gone rancid, the chemical sweetness of cheap mana-stimulants, and under it
all the particular sourness of too many people kept too poor for too long in too small a
space. Towers of stacked container-housing leaned over crooked lanes. Tarps sagged with
rainwater. A broken sign three streets off threw red light across the puddles in a slow,
sick pulse, and by that light he could see the district the way the boy had seen it
every day of his short life: a trap with the shape of a home.

And he could see something else, now, that the boy had never seen.

When he let his eyes settle — really settle, with the banked largeness lending them
something, with the cross-split of his pupils catching the red light and turning it
strange — the Hollows resolved into *flows*. Threads of faint power moving through it.
The mana the boy's memory had named: it ran in the gutters of the world like a second
rainfall, pooling where the desperate had spent it, thin and bruised in this poor place
but *there*, everywhere, a river the old world had never had and the new one was
drowning in without knowing. And where the threads tangled thickest, off east, past the
leaning towers, he could see a knot of them — a churning, wrong-feeling snarl in the
air, a place where the mana ran *the wrong way*, inward, hungry.

*A Gate,* the boy's memory supplied. *An unregistered one. The Tallyman's. They drive
the scavengers into it. Some come back. The debt is paid in what they carry out, or in
the not-coming-back.*

Xu Qing looked at it a long time, the rain running down his swollen face, his torn hands
loose and still at his sides, his cold mind already doing the only thing it had ever
truly done — the only thing it would do until whatever finally killed him stopped it for
good.

It counted.

It counted the hours of his window. It counted the predator's routine and where it
could be broken. It counted the river of power running unguarded through the gutters of
a world that did not yet know it had a thing in it for which there was no row in any
table. It counted the banked country of giants under his bones, and the bill that was
hidden somewhere he could not yet see, and the old grieving voice that was glad of him
for reasons of its own. It counted the exits — and for the first time in two lifetimes,
lying at the bottom of everything in a borrowed body in a foreign slum, it found that
there were more exits than it had expected, and that some of them led *up*, into a
largeness he would have to be very, very careful ever to use.

Far off, past the leaning towers, a door opened in one of the stacked container-homes,
and a man came out into the rain to start the day's collecting, with two more behind
him, unhurried, the way men move when they have never in their lives had a reason to
hurry.

*Dawn,* Xu Qing thought. *They come at dawn. The memory was right.*

He stepped back into the shadow of the rusted wall — into the one move he had always
trusted above every other, the small one, the unseen one, the patient one — and he
watched the Tallyman of the Hollows come to collect a debt from a boy who was no longer
there to pay it.

*Inventory,* the cold voice in him said, satisfied, complete. *And then the exits. And
then — when the arithmetic favors me, and not one second before — the rest.*

The rain came down on the Hollows, and on the rusted city, and on the small still shape
in the shadow whose pupils were crossed, and who had survived the end of time, and who
intended, with the unhurried patience of a man who had buried emperors, to survive the
morning too.
