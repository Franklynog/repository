# Prologue: The Light Past Time

*Xu Qing — final hour of the old world — age unrecorded, the last cultivator standing at the edge of everything*

There is a place where time ends.

Not the way a road ends, at a wall or a cliff or a sea, where a man may stand and look across at more world he simply cannot reach. Time ends the way a held breath ends — into a silence that was always underneath the sound, a silence so total that the word *silence* is already too loud for it. The cultivators of his age had a name for the country beyond that ending. They called it the **timescape**, and they spoke of it the way the living speak of the dead: rarely, and never after dark.

Xu Qing stood at the edge of it.

He was old now. He had let himself grow old, which was its own kind of discipline, because there had been centuries when growing old was the most dangerous thing a man could do and he had done it anyway, slowly, on purpose, the way he did everything — measuring each year against the next, spending nothing he could keep, keeping nothing he could not defend. His hair had gone the grey of cold ash. His hands, folded inside his sleeves, were steady. They had always been steady. That steadiness had outlived emperors.

Behind him lay the whole long road of his life, and it was a road paved end to end in caution. He had been born in the mud of the Sorrow Lake, a starving child in a starving district, a nobody so small the world had not bothered to learn his name. He had survived because he was patient where other children were brave, because he hid where they fought, because he counted the exits in every room before he counted the friends. He had clawed upward through a hundred ranks and a thousand graves that were meant to be his, and he had never once mistaken his own survival for safety. *Safety is a story the strong tell the weak,* he had decided, very young, in the dark, with someone else's blood drying on his hands. *There is only the next risk, and whether the arithmetic favors you.*

The arithmetic had favored him for a very long time.

It did not favor him now.

He looked into the place where time ended and he understood, with the cold clarity that had been the truest companion of his life, that there was no exit here. He had counted them. There were none. Every road he had ever walked had had a back door, a shadow to step into, a smaller move that bought a larger one — and this road had only its end. Whatever lay beyond the timescape did not have a back door, because it did not have a *back*. It had no behind and no before. It was the thing that *before* and *behind* were made out of.

A lesser man would have wept, or raged, or knelt. Xu Qing did the thing he had always done when the arithmetic turned against him and there was no smaller move to make.

He made the larger one.

He stepped forward, into the ending of time, because forward was the only direction left that he had not yet spent — and a cautious man, at the very last, is simply a man who has saved one move for when there is nothing else; and he had saved this one his whole life without ever admitting it, the move past the final boundary, the one with no return in its arithmetic at all.

The crossed pupils of his eyes — the strange split that had marked him since birth, two faint crosses in the dark of his stare, the thing the fortune-tellers of the Sorrow Lake had feared and the monks had coveted and he himself had simply *used*, the way he used everything — caught the last light there was and held it.

And then there was no light, because light is a thing that travels, and travel is a thing that takes time, and there was no more time.

He should have ended there. That is what the ending of time is *for*. He had made his peace with it the only way he made peace with anything: by accepting the figures and not arguing with them. *This is where I stop. The ledger closes. It was a long account, and I paid more than I was paid, and I leave nothing owed.* That was as close to a prayer as Xu Qing had ever come, and he did not believe anyone was listening, and he was not afraid.

That was when the light reached back.

It came from *outside* — outside the ending, outside the timescape, outside the whole shape of everything he had ever called real — and it was the wrong color. Not the gold of cultivation, not the white of the heavens, not the black of the abyss he had stared into so many times that he had stopped flinching. It was *purple*. A deep, bruised, sovereign purple, the color of a wound and the color of a crown, and it came *through* the place where nothing could come through, the way water comes through stone given enough patience, except that this had no patience at all. It was simply *there*, suddenly and absolutely, as if it had always had the right.

It found him in the nothing the way a hand finds a coin in the dark.

And it took him.

There was no pain, because pain takes time. There was no fear, because fear takes a future to be afraid *of*. There was only the cold arithmetic of his mind, working even now, even here, even as the purple light closed around the small still flame of what he was and *pulled* — working, and arriving, with the last clarity it would ever have in that world, at a single conclusion that he carried with him across the impossible gap like a knife carried in a sleeve:

*This is not my world's light. Something outside has reached in. And things that reach do so because they want.*

*Be careful,* he told himself, in the place past time, with no breath to say it and no time to say it in. *Whatever wants you — be careful.*

It was the last thing he thought as Xu Qing, and it was the first law of the man he would become.

---

He fell through a length of dark that had no length.

It was not falling, exactly, because falling needs a floor to fall toward and there was none — but his mind, which had spent an entire existence translating the unsurvivable into terms it could survive, called it falling, and so he fell. Through purple. Through the wrong-colored deep. Past shapes that were almost there and almost gone, vast hanging shapes like drowned mountains, like the bones of something that had been bigger than reason, ranked in the dark on either side of his fall as if he were dropping down the nave of a temple built for a congregation of giants.

And in the dark, as he fell, a voice found him.

It was not the purple light. The light had no voice; the light was only *want*, pure and patient and old. The voice was something else — something *inside* the fall, or inside *him*, woken by the falling the way deep water is woken by a stone. It was enormous. Not loud. *Enormous*, the way a mountain is enormous when you stand at its foot and tip your head back and back and the top is still not there. It was a voice with grief in it, and pride, and the particular exhaustion of a thing that has waited so long it has forgotten what it waits for and remembers only that it is still waiting.

It spoke two words into the place where Xu Qing was no longer Xu Qing and not yet anyone, and the two words went through him like the first light of a country he had never seen and somehow already mourned.

***...little prince.***

And then the falling found a floor, after all.

The floor was a body.

He went into it the way a sword goes into a sheath that was cut for it — not gently, not kindly, but *correctly*, every part of him matching every part of the dark wet ruined thing he was suddenly inside of, as if the body had been waiting his whole death for exactly his shape. He felt its pain become his pain. He felt its cold become his cold. He felt a heart that had been about to stop remember, reluctantly, how to keep going, and take up the work again like an old laborer dragged back to a job he hated.

And he felt — underneath the pain and the cold and the rain that was somehow falling, real rain, the first real rain of a real new world drumming on his ruined new face — he felt the body's own life, its short cruel starving seventeen years, rise up to meet the long cold cunning centuries of his soul.

He braced for them to tear at each other. Two hard lives, two sets of scars, two ledgers of grief — they should have fought. He had seen souls shatter on the rocks of a borrowed body; he had *used* that, more than once, against enemies who tried to live past their deaths.

They did not fight.

They *fit*.

The body's despair found his caution and recognized it. The body's hunger found his patience and lay down beside it. The body's long memory of being small, of being beaten, of being *nothing the world bothered to name* — it found, in the deep cold arithmetic of the man who had survived two existences by being exactly those things, not an enemy but a *brother*. The seam between them closed without a scar. Where Xu Qing ended and the dying boy began, there was, after a single endless instant, no line at all.

*A perfect merge,* some cold corner of him noted, even now, even here, lying in the rain in the alley of a world he had not yet opened his eyes upon. *Too perfect. The light chose this body for me, or chose me for this body. Either way — be careful. A thing that fits this well was measured first.*

The rain came down.

Somewhere far above the alley, past the rain and the rusted sky of a city he had no name for yet, the purple light withdrew, its want satisfied for now, leaving behind only the thing it had wanted and the long cold work of finding out why.

And in the wet dark, in a body that had been minutes from a pauper's death, a pair of plain dark eyes opened — and in each of them, faint as a watermark, faint as a promise, faint as a debt not yet come due, there was the cross-shaped split of a pupil that had looked into the ending of time and come back out the other side.

Xu Qing breathed in the air of the world of the Gates.

It tasted of rust, and rain, and someone else's blood, and — very faintly, under all of it, so faint he would not understand it for a long time — of a country of giants, mourning.

*Inventory,* the cold voice in him said, the way it had said it ten thousand times across two worlds, the way it would say it until whatever finally killed him. *Inventory, and then the exits.*

He had survived the end of time.

Now he had to survive the morning.
